Questions to Ask During a Roofing Inspection

Learn what to ask during a roofing inspection in Millersville, MD so you understand what was checked and why it matters.

Clear questions help a homeowner understand the findings of a roofing inspection. The value of an inspection comes from understanding which parts of the roof system were actually examined, what evidence was used to reach a conclusion, and how any findings connect to a recommended repair or replacement. Without asking, a homeowner may receive a summary of conclusions with no sense of how those conclusions were reached or which areas were left unexamined.

This article focuses on the questions worth asking a roofing professional during or after an inspection in Millersville, Maryland. It does not instruct homeowners to climb a roof or inspect it themselves; ground-level and indoor observations, paired with direct questions to the inspector, are the safer and more practical approach for most households.

What areas of the roof were actually examined?

A thorough answer should identify specific components, not just a general impression of roof condition. According to GAF's educational overview of roof components, a roof system includes decking, underlayment, flashing, shingles or other covering materials, and ventilation elements, and each plays a distinct role in keeping water out of the structure (GAF roofing components guide). An inspector who only mentions shingle appearance may not have evaluated flashing at chimneys, skylights, or sidewalls, where leaks often originate.

It is reasonable to ask whether the inspection included a walk of the attic space, since attic observations can reveal moisture staining, daylight gaps, or ventilation issues that are not visible from the ground or from photos of the roof surface alone. If the inspector did not access the attic, ask why, and whether that limits the conclusions being offered.

Homeowners should also ask whether valleys, eaves, and any roof penetrations such as vent pipes were checked individually, since these are common sources of leaks that a general visual scan can miss.

What evidence supports the stated findings?

Photographs, moisture readings, or specific descriptions of damage location and extent are the kind of evidence that supports a credible inspection finding. A vague statement that the roof is in poor condition is less useful than a description tying specific damage to a specific location, such as lifted shingle tabs near a particular slope or staining beneath a specific valley.

Asking to see photos taken during the inspection, and asking the inspector to point out exactly where on the roof each photo was taken, helps a homeowner connect findings to the physical roof rather than taking a summary at face value. If moisture intrusion is suspected, ask how that conclusion was reached and whether it is based on visible staining, a moisture meter reading, or another observable sign.

GAF's guide to roof repair notes that identifying the specific cause and location of damage is a necessary step before deciding on an appropriate repair, since different types of damage call for different scopes of work (GAF roof repair guide). Asking how a finding translates into a recommended scope keeps the conversation grounded in what was actually observed.

How does this finding affect a repair or replacement recommendation?

A useful inspection report connects specific findings to a specific recommendation, rather than presenting a recommendation as a standalone conclusion. If a contractor recommends replacement, ask which specific findings led to that recommendation versus a more limited repair, and whether the recommendation would change if only one or two of those findings were present.

It is also worth asking whether the inspector found any conditions that could not be fully assessed from available vantage points, such as decking condition beneath existing shingles, which typically cannot be confirmed until the roof covering is removed. Flagging this upfront helps a homeowner anticipate that a written estimate may include a provision for how concealed conditions, if discovered, would be documented and addressed.

Finally, ask whether the inspection findings and any photos will be provided in writing. A written record gives a homeowner something concrete to compare against a second opinion or to reference later if questions arise about the scope of recommended work.

Questions this guide answers

Should I ask to see photos from the roof inspection?

Yes, asking to see inspection photos helps connect stated findings to specific locations on the roof rather than relying on a general summary. Request that the inspector identify where each photo was taken. This is particularly useful when comparing a repair recommendation against a replacement recommendation.

Does a roofing inspection usually include the attic?

Not always, so it is worth asking directly whether attic access was part of the inspection. Attic observations can reveal moisture staining or ventilation issues not visible from the roof surface. If the attic was not checked, ask how that affects the confidence of the stated findings.

Can an inspector tell me the condition of the decking without removing shingles?

Generally no, since decking is typically concealed beneath the existing roof covering until it is removed. An inspector can note signs suggestive of possible decking issues, such as sagging or staining, but full confirmation usually waits until tear-off. Ask how your estimate addresses this possibility in writing.
