California headache and migraine specialist care planning: Maintenance Decisions

Maintain an agreed care plan by tracking observations, assigning follow-up tasks, organizing records, and preserving escalation contacts.

Maintenance begins after a clinician and patient have agreed on what will happen next. The task is not to improvise changes; it is to preserve an accurate record, complete assigned steps, notice questions that need professional review, and keep escalation routes usable. This guide helps California adults build a calm follow-up system for headache care without recommending a medicine, procedure, device, supplement, infusion, injection, or behavioural approach. It is informational, not medical advice, and was reviewed on August 24, 2026. Emergency guidance remains separate: call 911 for CDC stroke signs or other sudden, severe, new, or unusual neurologic symptoms.

Turn the visit summary into an accountable care map

Before details fade, write the plan in plain language. Record the clinician’s stated assessment, what decisions were made, what remains uncertain, and which actions were assigned. For each task, identify an owner and target date: patient requests prior imaging, office sends an order, health plan reviews authorization, pharmacy clarifies a claim, another clinician performs an examination, or the treating professional reviews results. An action without an owner is a common source of lost follow-up. List the care team by role rather than assuming everyone shares the same record. Include primary care, headache-focused care, nursing contacts, pharmacy, imaging or laboratory sites, procedure or infusion facilities when relevant, behavioural support, urgent pathways, and the emergency department boundary. Note which professional coordinates the overall plan and where outside reports must be sent. Save the agreed monitoring instructions exactly as provided. Include what to observe, how to report it, whom to contact for adverse events, and when the next review occurs. Do not create your own thresholds or change treatment based on a generic tracker. If the written summary conflicts with what you remember, ask the responsible office to clarify.

Track useful observations without turning the diary into a verdict

A practical diary can record date and onset, duration, associated symptoms, functional effect, activity interruption, and recovery. It may also include clinician-directed measures and neutral observations about what happened afterward. Use consistent terms and leave uncertain entries marked uncertain. A blank field is better than reconstructed precision. Keep the diary proportionate. Daily logging may be appropriate when specifically requested, but elaborate tracking can become burdensome or misleading. Ask the clinician which fields influence the next decision and what review period is useful. The record cannot establish a diagnosis, prove causation, determine eligibility, or demonstrate that one category should be selected. Maintain a separate medicine history. Record current items, the prescribing or managing professional, refill questions, and any instructions received. For past items, preserve approximate dates and reasons for discontinuation. Never use the maintenance worksheet as permission to alter dosing, stop suddenly, restart an old medicine, borrow another person’s medicine, or combine products. Direct safety and interaction questions to the accountable clinician or pharmacist.

Schedule reviews around decisions and keep escape routes visible

Every follow-up date should have a purpose. Examples include reviewing new records, discussing monitoring observations, checking an unresolved risk question, deciding whether further examination is needed, or coordinating an outside service. Before the appointment, summarize developments since the last review and identify the top two unanswered questions. This creates continuity without repeating the complete history. Preserve three contact routes: routine questions, time-sensitive non-emergency concerns, and emergencies. Confirm portal monitoring hours, backup telephone details, after-hours instructions, and the location for an in-person assessment if remote care is insufficient. Administrative contact channels should not be mistaken for clinical triage. If the established pattern changes materially, ask for updated direction rather than waiting automatically for the next scheduled review. Review the system periodically for stale information. Recheck clinician credentials through official California registries when appropriate, verify insurance and authorization details for new services, update allergies and current medicines, remove duplicate records, and confirm that caregivers have the right emergency information. End each maintenance cycle with four questions: what changed, what was completed, what remains unresolved, and who owns the next step? The goal is a reliable record and a clear handoff, not a promise that attacks will improve or that any intervention will produce a particular outcome.

Questions this guide answers

What should happen immediately after a care-planning visit?

Record the agreed decisions, unresolved questions, assigned tasks, owners, deadlines, monitoring instructions, and escalation contacts.

Can diary observations prove that a care category caused a change?

No. They can support a clinician conversation but do not establish diagnosis, causation, eligibility, or outcome.

When should a scheduled review be moved forward?

Use the clinician’s instructions and seek updated direction when the pattern changes, an adverse-event question arises, or an assigned task reveals a new concern.
