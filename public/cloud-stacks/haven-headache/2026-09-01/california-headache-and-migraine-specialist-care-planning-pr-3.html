California headache and migraine specialist care planning: Preparation Checklist 2

Prepare a compact specialist packet containing a symptom timeline, medicine history, records index, verified clinician details, and prioritized questions.

Preparation is most useful when it reduces reconstruction during the visit without turning the patient into a diagnostician. The decision here is what belongs in a concise appointment packet and what should remain available only as backup. The eight-part framework below prioritizes dated observations, complete medicine information, source-labeled records, and unanswered questions. It does not tell anyone to start, stop, or alter treatment. This independent worksheet was reviewed August 24, 2026; it provides general information, not medical advice.

Build the clinical summary in four parts

1. Write a one-sentence visit objective. Examples are understanding a changed pattern, reviewing an established plan, or clarifying next assessment steps. Avoid stating an assumed diagnosis as the objective. If the current problem is sudden, severe, new, or accompanied by unusual neurologic changes, preparation for a routine appointment is not the priority; seek emergency help when warning signs indicate it. 2. Create a symptom-history snapshot. Record approximate onset, pattern over time, attack duration, associated symptoms, changes from the person’s usual experience, and effect on ordinary function. Use migraine for the disease and migraine attack for an episode when that diagnosis is established or being discussed. Do not use a diary score to declare what condition is present. 3. Make a dated timeline. Include major changes, emergency or urgent assessments, relevant pregnancies or hormonal changes if the person wishes to discuss them, prior clinician evaluations, and important testing. Keep interpretation separate from fact. “Report dated June 3 was described as normal” is clearer than an unsupported conclusion about what the test rules out. 4. Prepare the medicine record. List current prescription and nonprescription medicines, supplements, allergies, and past trials. For each, record the name, form, reason it was used, approximate dates, the patient’s recollection of effect, and why it ended if known. Bring container labels or a current pharmacy list when practical. Do not omit a medicine because it seems unrelated, and do not change it merely to prepare for the visit.

Add four operational elements

5. Build a records index rather than an indiscriminate pile. List prior notes, imaging reports, laboratory results, discharge papers, and procedure records with dates and source organizations. Mark each item as attached, requested, unavailable, or patient summary only. Ask whether images themselves are needed in addition to written reports. Share records through a secure method specified by the recipient. 6. Verify people and roles. Record the scheduled professional’s full name, role, organization, and visit type. Check a physician through the Medical Board of California and a nursing credential through the Board of Registered Nursing. Save the verification date and distinguish the official record from a biography or appointment confirmation. 7. Prepare a care-team map. Include primary care, relevant specialists, pharmacy, procedure sites, and emergency contacts. Note who currently handles refills, monitoring, test follow-up, and urgent questions. This exposes ownership gaps without assuming that the new clinician will take over every task. 8. Prioritize questions. Put the three questions that would most affect the next decision at the top. Useful themes include diagnostic uncertainty, need for examination or testing, acute and preventive goals, risks and interactions, pregnancy considerations, monitoring, alternatives, and escalation instructions. Place administrative questions—network status, referrals, authorization, records receipt, and billing identity—on a separate page so they reach the party able to answer.

Compress, check, and use the packet

Aim for a one- or two-page summary with an indexed appendix. Use large readable type, plain headings, generous spacing, and low-glare colors. A digital copy should have descriptive filenames and accessible text, not only photographs. For a virtual visit, test audio, camera, power, connection, privacy, and the backup contact route. Reduce screen brightness if needed and ask about accessibility accommodations in advance. Run a final accuracy check. Confirm names and dates, label uncertain memories as approximate, remove duplicate records, and correct conflicts rather than hiding them. Do not include another person’s information. Decide whether a support person will participate and obtain agreement about what that person may hear or contribute. During the visit, use the packet as a map rather than reading every line. Ask the clinician to correct misunderstandings. Record the agreed next step, what remains uncertain, who is responsible, warning signs, adverse-event contacts, and the next review point. If recording the encounter is desired, ask permission and follow applicable rules; written notes are the default. Afterward, compare the visit summary with personal notes and request clarification of material discrepancies. Store the packet securely and update only the fields that changed. Preparation succeeds when it leaves both patient and clinician with an accurate shared history and an executable follow-up plan, not when it predicts the final clinical decision.

Questions this guide answers

Should every available record be placed in the main summary?

No. Keep the summary concise and use a dated index for supporting material. Ask the receiving practice which reports, images, or notes it needs.

Should medicines be changed before the appointment?

Not on the basis of this checklist. Record current and past use accurately and direct change questions to the responsible clinician.
