Put Migraine and Migraine Attacks in the Right Parts of Your Notes

Organize clinician-confirmed history, observed episodes, and open questions so your records clearly distinguish what is known from what needs review.

Useful care notes keep three things visible. One is the wording a clinician has documented. A second is what you remember about a particular episode. A third is the question you still want answered. Those items can fit on the same page if they have separate labels. Use migraine for the disease and migraine attack for an episode in that context. When a clinician has yet to assess an event, write what happened and keep the question open. The structure gives the person reading your notes information they can evaluate while preserving your own understanding of why you need help.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. Use this guide to prepare questions for your appointment. Bring questions about your individual circumstances to a qualified clinician.

Preserve the words from the person who documented them

If you have a visit summary, copy the relevant wording into a short history line and add the date and office that supplied it. Include any qualification such as “under evaluation” or “discussed as a possibility.” A shared record can contain old questions, past diagnoses, resolved concerns, and pending tests. The label and date help the next reader place a term in context. When a clinician explains a change in your history, ask them how they would describe it in future visits and whether a written copy is available. If two sources use different words, keep both with their dates and ask which wording reflects the current assessment.

Your record can accurately show that a question was raised without trying to resolve it yourself.

Describe an episode in observable terms

Record when it began as best you can and what it interrupted. An entry might refer to leaving a meeting, asking someone else to drive, or cancelling a task. Describe anything you noticed in ordinary language and add context such as what you were doing at the time. If someone else observed a change, record their version separately and say who supplied it. Save questions about meaning for the care conversation. Distinguishing what you observed from what you think caused it can help the clinician review both. For episodes already discussed with your care team, follow the recording guidance they gave you.

Ask for a simpler template if a detailed diary takes time you cannot reasonably spare.

Use respectful language for people and plans

A person living with migraine may prefer a different way of describing their own experience. If you help someone prepare for an appointment, ask what language they want used in the note and what information they want shared. The person may value help compiling dates while preferring to discuss symptoms privately. Agree on what you can answer on their behalf before the appointment. When writing a question for the clinician, make the subject explicit. “How should I record the events we will review next month?” invites a response you can apply.

Put any instructions that come back into a separate current-plan section. That keeps a personal journal, a verified medical record, and a care decision easy to identify if several people are supporting one visit.

Bring a short list of words to clarify

Mark terms you have heard in a consultation, message, or insurance discussion that still affect a decision. Next to each term, write the action it concerns. For example, an office may use a phrase when discussing records, while your insurer may use the same phrase in relation to authorization. Ask the appropriate recipient to explain it in the context of your next step. During the visit, repeat back the part you need to act on and ask where you can read it again. When you receive a written answer, file it with the visit date.

If the answer affects records sent to another office, ask which record should travel and how the receiving practice can confirm that it has received the document.
