Lower Mainland allied health provider and rehabilitation plannin: Questions Before Booking

A pre-booking checklist for verifying provider identity, occupational-therapy scope, appointment purpose, consent, records, costs, and access.

Before booking occupational therapy or another allied-health appointment, decide whether the clinic can answer the essential identity, purpose, process, privacy, access, and payment questions. A booking form may collect information, but it cannot make those decisions for you. This checklist does not determine whether occupational therapy is suitable or provide a treatment plan. Reviewed August 24, 2026.

Identify the provider and appointment purpose

Ask for the full name and profession of the person who is expected to conduct the initial appointment. If the booking is for occupational therapy, verify the individual through the correct public registry and confirm that the record matches. Do not assume a clinic name, multidisciplinary affiliation, online profile, or receipt category proves current registration. If the service may be delivered by an assistant, student, contractor, kinesiologist, or another professional, ask who supervises, who is accountable, and whose designation will appear in records and receipts. Next, describe your question in functional terms. You might want to discuss daily activities, workplace demands, home routines, accessibility, fatigue management questions, equipment claims, or coordination after surgery, but those examples do not establish professional suitability. Ask the provider how the proposed appointment relates to the concern, what is outside their role, and whether medical instructions or a referral should be obtained first. Request a description of the first visit: interview, observation, physical activity, environmental discussion, forms, measurements, or contact with another person. Ask which parts are optional, what alternatives may exist, and whether you can pause to ask questions. If the clinic cannot identify the practitioner or explain the appointment’s basic purpose, do not let scheduling pressure replace clarification.

Clarify information, consent, and coordination

Ask what personal and health information the clinic needs before booking and why each category is relevant. Avoid sending a complete chart by default. Determine how documents are transmitted, stored, accessed, corrected, and shared. If an employer, insurer, lawyer, family member, physician, surgeon, or another rehabilitation provider is involved, ask exactly what communication is proposed, who receives it, and what authorization is required. A broad consent form should not substitute for a specific discussion. Request time to read forms, and ask how consent may be limited or withdrawn, subject to an explanation of consequences. If an occupational therapist may prepare a report, clarify its audience, purpose, expected content, completion process, and fee. Ask whether you can review factual details and how errors are corrected. For multidisciplinary care, list each provider’s role and the question they are handling. Ask who will notice conflicting recommendations or duplicate assessments. Do not assume the booking clinic is coordinating simply because several disciplines share a location. If language interpretation, a support person, sensory accommodation, accessible entry, virtual attendance, or extra communication time is needed, ask what can be arranged before selecting a date. Keep a record of the answer and the staff member who provided it.

Confirm costs, timing, and booking conditions

Request the current initial and follow-up fees, expected duration, optional costs, report or form charges, cancellation rules, late-arrival consequences, and payment methods. If a package is offered, ask about expiry, refunds, transfers, unused visits, and what happens if the professional plan changes. Never interpret direct billing as guaranteed reimbursement. Contact the insurer or claim organization with the exact profession and provider; verify referral requirements, authorization, eligible expenses, maximums, deductibles, user fees, submission dates, and responsibility for unpaid balances. In an ICBC matter, use current ICBC guidance and record the claim-specific answer. Ask the clinic for its billing process separately. Confirm the address, appointment format, arrival instructions, parking or transit considerations, physical accessibility, and what to bring. Availability should be treated as an operational fact that may change, not a quality signal. Before accepting the slot, read back seven answers: named provider, verified profession, stated purpose, expected process, consent choices, known cost, and records needed. Mark unresolved items and decide whether they are minor enough to settle at the appointment or important enough to resolve first. New, severe, rapidly changing, or otherwise concerning symptoms should be directed to appropriate medical or emergency assessment rather than held for a routine booking. After confirmation, save the date, cancellation deadline, clinic contact, fee quotation, registry-check date, and priority questions. This consumer checklist is informational, not medical, legal, or insurance advice. It must remain private and non-indexable until clinical, factual, citation, media, link, accessibility, and schema reviews pass.

Questions this guide answers

What must I know before booking occupational therapy?

Know the provider’s identity and registry result, appointment purpose and process, consent choices, information-sharing plan, access arrangements, fees, and payer conditions.

Does a multidisciplinary clinic automatically coordinate my records?

No. Ask who is responsible, what will be shared, which consent is required, and how conflicting or duplicated work will be addressed.

Can I wait for the appointment if symptoms become concerning?

A routine booking should not delay suitable medical or emergency assessment for new, severe, rapidly changing, or otherwise concerning symptoms.
