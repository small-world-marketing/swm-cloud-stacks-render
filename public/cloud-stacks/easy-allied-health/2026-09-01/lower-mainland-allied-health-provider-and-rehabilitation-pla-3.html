Lower Mainland allied health provider and rehabilitation planning: Preparation Checklist

Prepare a concise appointment brief covering goals, observations, history, records, access needs, consent questions, and follow-up responsibilities.

Good preparation gives a provider relevant facts without forcing you to diagnose yourself. This decision framework helps readers decide what to collect, what to summarize, and what can wait. It is designed for routine allied-health appointments after urgent concerns have been directed to suitable medical care. Reviewed August 24, 2026; the material is educational and not personalized clinical guidance.

Create a one-page appointment brief

At the top, write the appointment date, clinic, named provider, profession, and the question you hope the visit will address. Describe changes in function using concrete examples: an activity that became difficult, circumstances that alter it, and how it affects work, home duties, sleep, mobility, recreation, or caregiving. Use your own observations and avoid assigning a condition. Add when the issue began, whether it followed an event or procedure, and whether it is stable, improving, worsening, or variable. List prior assessments and instructions with dates and authors. Include relevant surgeries, injuries, medical conditions, allergies, medications, devices, and current providers, but do not stop or change medication based on this worksheet. Bring referral documents, imaging or reports only when requested or reasonably connected to the appointment; ask before sending an entire chart. Note communication, cultural, sensory, mobility, privacy, or accessibility needs so the clinic can explain what arrangements are possible. If another person will attend, decide what role you want them to have. Prepare two functional goals and no more than five priority questions. Examples include what the assessment involves, which parts are optional, who will be present, and what information would prompt contact with a medical professional. A short accurate page is more usable than an unfiltered archive.

Confirm the appointment conditions

Before travelling, verify the location, arrival procedure, accessibility details, current fee, cancellation policy, expected duration, and payment method. Ask whether the visit is in person or remote and what privacy limitations apply to the chosen format. Confirm the person providing the service and independently check the correct public registry when the profession is regulated. Ask whether clothing changes, physical contact, movement, equipment, forms, or a support person may be involved. These questions help you prepare; they do not create consent in advance. Consent should remain voluntary and specific as the appointment unfolds. If insurance or a claim is involved, bring the required identifiers but minimize unnecessary disclosure. Check benefit conditions with the payer rather than assuming that a referral, provider listing, or clinic submission guarantees reimbursement. For ICBC matters, record the claim number, crash date, adjuster or recovery specialist contact, and any written authorization or correspondence relevant to current rules. Ask the clinic whether it bills ICBC, whether user fees might arise, and what happens if payment is declined. Pack identification only if legitimately required, a medication list, relevant instructions, a notebook, and any prescribed mobility or communication aid you ordinarily use. Leave valuables and unrelated originals at home. Save secure copies of irreplaceable records.

Plan how the visit will end

Reserve space on the page for four closing answers: what the provider observed, what was proposed, what you consented to, and what happens next. Ask for unfamiliar terms to be restated. If a recommendation includes a device, modality, repeated visits, or communication with another party, ask about purpose, alternatives, material risks, cost, duration, evidence relevant to the claim, and stopping or review criteria. You may request time to decide when delay is clinically reasonable. Confirm who will send reports, which records you receive, and what needs your authorization. Do not assume the clinic will update every member of a multidisciplinary team. Afterward, record questions that remain unresolved and any unexpected event in neutral language, including date, timing, and who was informed. Do not convert the record into a self-generated clinical score. Store clinical notes, consent documents, receipts, and payer correspondence in separate labelled folders linked by date. If new or concerning symptoms appear, do not wait for a routine follow-up merely because it is already booked; seek suitable assessment based on urgency. Reuse the brief only after updating medication, medical instructions, provider identity, and the main decision. Preparation is successful when it improves the accuracy of the conversation, not when every blank is filled. Keep this draft private pending clinical, factual, citation, accessibility, media, link, and schema approval.

Questions this guide answers

Should I bring my complete medical chart?

Usually begin with a concise relevant summary and documents requested by the provider. Ask before transferring broad records that may contain unnecessary information.

Does completing intake paperwork mean I consent to every service?

No. Consent should remain informed, voluntary, specific, and open to questions or changes during the appointment.

What should my preparation sheet emphasize?

Emphasize the current functional concern, relevant history and instructions, medication information, access needs, and a short set of priority questions.
