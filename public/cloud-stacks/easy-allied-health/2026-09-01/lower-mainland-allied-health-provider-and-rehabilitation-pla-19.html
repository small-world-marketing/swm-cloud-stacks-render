Lower Mainland allied health provider and rehabilitation planning: Preparation Checklist 3

Prepare a concise appointment packet with verified provider details, current records, consent questions, practical constraints, and follow-up notes.

Good preparation is selective. Carrying every document you possess can obscure the current question, while arriving with no dates, restrictions, or medication information can make the conversation harder. This checklist helps adults assemble a compact, current appointment packet for allied-health care in the Lower Mainland. It applies whether the planned discussion concerns daily function, mobility, work, post-surgical planning, counselling coordination, or an insurance claim. It does not tell you what service to obtain or replace instructions from a clinician. Escalate urgent, severe, or quickly changing symptoms for appropriate medical assessment instead of waiting for a routine visit. Reviewed on 2026-08-24 and provided for information, not medical advice.

Before booking: verify the appointment and define its purpose

□ Write one sentence describing why you are seeking the appointment. Focus on the activity, responsibility, restriction, or question involved rather than diagnosing yourself. Add two or three priorities and a brief timeline of relevant events. □ Obtain the practitioner’s full name and professional title. Locate the individual in the public register maintained by the regulator for that profession. Check that identifying details correspond to the intended person. If the entry is unclear, ask the regulator; a clinic affiliation is not a substitute. □ Ask what the first visit is designed to include. Will it involve history-taking, assessment, education, a proposed intervention, forms, or coordination? Who else may participate? What preparation does the provider request? Do not assume a named modality will be used or is suitable. □ Confirm administrative facts directly with the clinic: current fee, deposits, cancellation rules, accessibility information, expected documentation, communication accommodations, and possible charges for reports, supplies, or forms. Ask what “direct billing” means in that clinic’s process. Separately confirm eligibility, limits, referrals, and authorizations with the payer. □ Identify the responsible source for restrictions. Bring current instructions from a surgeon, physician, or other accountable clinician when relevant. Note who can revise them and the scheduled review point. An allied-health appointment should not silently replace another professional’s restriction. □ Decide whether a support person or interpreter is desired and permitted. Ask how privacy and consent will work if someone joins. Confirm transportation and caregiving arrangements without assuming appointment length, availability, or physical demands.

Build a small, current record packet

□ Create a one-page timeline with dates for the current concern, important changes, surgery or injury if applicable, previous assessments, and key communications. Use neutral observations. Avoid assigning causes or severity scores unless a responsible clinician asked you to use a particular tool. □ Prepare an accurate medication and supplement list showing names, amounts as recorded on labels or instructions, timing, and prescribing source. Include allergies or adverse reactions that clinicians have asked you to report. Do not change medication because of this worksheet; take medication questions to the appropriate prescriber or pharmacist. □ List the current care team, professional roles, contact channels, and the reason each person is involved. Mark who should receive information and whether permission has been given. Shared premises do not guarantee shared records. □ Bring only relevant referrals, operative or discharge instructions, reports, imaging summaries, claim correspondence, authorizations, or prior care summaries. Ask whether copies are sufficient and how electronic records can be transferred securely. Keep originals unless an authorized recipient specifically requires them. □ For an ICBC matter, organize the claim number, dates, provider correspondence, receipts, benefit statements, and any approvals as distinct items. Review ICBC’s current published information and confirm claim-specific questions with ICBC. Do not infer entitlement from another person’s experience or from a provider-search result. □ Place your questions at the front. Useful topics include scope, who performs each part, choices, material risks, uncertainty, alternatives, privacy, reporting, fees, and what would require another professional’s input. Leave space to record answers and unresolved items.

During and after: preserve consent and useful follow-up

□ At the visit, confirm the plan before any assessment or service begins. Ask for unfamiliar terms to be explained. Consent should address the actual proposal and may be paused or withdrawn; signing paperwork does not prevent later questions. □ State current restrictions, accessibility needs, relevant changes, and the most important functional concern. If information has changed since booking, identify the update rather than relying on an old intake form. □ Record the next action, its owner, and any deadline. Distinguish provider advice from clinic administration and payer requirements. Note whether a report will be created, who will receive it, its purpose, likely charge, and the permission governing disclosure. □ Keep receipts, invoices, claim submissions, consent documents, instructions, referrals, reports, and personal notes in separate dated folders. Correct your own summary when needed without changing original records. Save confirmation of correction requests. □ Afterward, note function in concrete terms, questions, unexpected or adverse events, and whether instructions are understandable and feasible. Contact the appropriate provider about concerns; do not use a personal log to grade treatment success or diagnose a reaction. □ Review the packet before the next appointment. Remove duplicates, mark obsolete restrictions as superseded without destroying them, update medications and providers, and carry forward unresolved questions. If a new warning sign arises, switch from routine preparation to appropriate medical or emergency guidance. The checklist is complete when the next decision and responsible contact are clear—not when every box contains a document.

Questions this guide answers

Should I bring my entire health record?

Usually, preparation is more useful when it is selective. Ask which records are relevant, how to transfer them securely, and whether copies or concise summaries are sufficient.

What belongs at the front of the packet?

Place the appointment purpose, current restrictions, priority questions, medication list, and essential timeline where they are easy to review.

Can my personal progress log diagnose a problem?

No. Record concrete observations, questions, and unexpected events, then take concerns to the appropriate professional.
