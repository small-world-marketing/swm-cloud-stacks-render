Prepare for a Home Visit: Access, Space, Privacy, Support, and Contacts

A practical checklist for preparing access, space, privacy, mobility aids, pets, support people, and emergency contacts before any mobile health-program home visit.

Preparing for a mobile rehabilitation or healthy-aging home visit is a logistics decision, not a medical one. Before any provider arrives, a household can reduce confusion and wasted time by confirming who can get in, where the session will happen, who else will be present, and what happens if something goes wrong. This checklist focuses on practical readiness rather than any specific program, provider, or diagnosis. It applies whether the visit relates to a longevity program, a mobility-and-balance program, an arthritis-focused program, or another mobile service a reader is still evaluating.

Confirm access and arrival logistics

Confirm, in advance, exactly how a provider will reach the home and where the session will take place once inside. This means checking building access codes, buzzer or intercom instructions, visitor parking rules, elevator availability, and whether a support person needs to meet the provider at a door or lobby. Households in multi-unit buildings around Port Coquitlam and elsewhere in Metro Vancouver often discover access friction only on the day of a visit, so writing these details down ahead of time avoids delays. It also helps to confirm what identification or appointment confirmation the provider may bring, since this is a separate question from whether that provider is currently licensed to practise, which is verified through the applicable professional registry rather than through the visit logistics themselves.

If a unit number, gate code, or delivery instruction is unusual, share it directly with whoever confirmed the appointment rather than assuming it is already on file. Clear arrival logistics reduce wasted appointment time and let the actual visit focus on its intended purpose rather than on finding the right door.

Set up a usable, private space

Choose a room or area with enough clear floor space for movement, a stable chair or surface if needed, and good lighting before the visit begins. Many home-based assessments or program sessions involve some walking, standing, or positioning, so clearing rugs, cords, and furniture obstacles in that specific area matters more than tidying the whole home. Privacy is a separate and equally important consideration: decide in advance whether conversations about symptoms, history, or goals should happen in a closed room away from shared living spaces, especially in households with roommates, extended family, or young children present.

If mobility aids such as a cane, walker, or wheelchair are normally used, keep them in the chosen space rather than elsewhere in the home, since retrieving them mid-visit interrupts the flow of an assessment. Households with pets should also plan ahead, either by securing the animal in another room or confirming with the provider beforehand whether a calm pet can safely remain present, since this varies by comfort level and household rather than by any fixed rule.

Decide who else should be present

Think through, before the visit, whether a support person, family member, or caregiver should be present for all or part of the session, and communicate that preference clearly. Some readers prefer a trusted person in the room to help recall details afterward, ask follow-up questions, or simply provide reassurance, particularly for a first visit with a new provider or an unfamiliar program category. Others may prefer private time to speak openly about symptoms, function, or concerns without a family member present, which is a reasonable and common choice. Either approach is valid, but deciding in advance avoids an awkward conversation at the door.

If a support person cannot attend in person, consider whether they should be reachable by phone during the visit to help with questions about history, medication changes, or prior appointments, especially if the person receiving the visit has memory or communication difficulties. Households coordinating care across multiple family members may also want to agree beforehand on who will take notes or follow up afterward, so that information from the visit does not rely on a single person's memory.

Prepare emergency contacts and a fallback plan

Write down at least one emergency contact name and phone number and place it somewhere visible before the visit, such as near the entryway or in the room being used. This is basic household preparedness rather than a program-specific requirement, and it matters regardless of which provider or service category is involved. Confirm who should be called if a scheduled visit needs to be rescheduled on short notice, and keep that contact information separate from the emergency contact so the two purposes are not confused. If the household relies on a specific entry method, such as a lockbox code or a neighbor holding a spare key, document that alongside the emergency contact information rather than relying on memory.

It is also worth confirming, separately from visit logistics, what the household would do in a genuine medical emergency during a session, since a home visit for a wellness or rehabilitation program is not an emergency service and urgent symptoms should always be directed to appropriate emergency care rather than handled through the scheduled appointment itself.

Questions this guide answers

Who should be present during a home visit?

This is a personal choice: some readers prefer a support person present to help recall details and ask questions, while others prefer privacy to discuss symptoms openly. Deciding in advance and communicating the preference to the provider avoids confusion at the start of the visit. Households coordinating care across family members may also want to agree on who takes notes afterward.

What should be clarified about pets before a home visit?

Decide in advance whether a pet will be secured in another room or can remain present, and confirm this with the provider beforehand since comfort levels vary. This is a logistics question separate from the content of the visit itself. Clarifying it ahead of time avoids last-minute disruption when the provider arrives.

Is preparing for a home visit the same as verifying a provider's credentials?

No, these are separate questions. Preparing access, space, and contacts is a logistics task, while confirming that a physical therapist or physician is currently licensed requires checking the applicable public registry directly rather than assuming a home visit implies verification.
