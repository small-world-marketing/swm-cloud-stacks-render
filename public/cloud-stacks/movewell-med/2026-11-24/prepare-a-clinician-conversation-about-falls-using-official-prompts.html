Prepare a Clinician Conversation About Falls Using Official Prompts

Use official fall-prevention prompts to organize a clinician conversation about falls risk, without turning the material into a self-diagnosis or exercise plan.

Deciding how to raise a fall or near-fall with a clinician is different from deciding whether you are at risk. This article helps Metro Vancouver readers use publicly available fall-prevention guidance to organize what to say, not to score their own risk or select exercises. The decision here is about preparation: which details matter, which questions to ask, and when to treat a fall as urgent rather than routine. A British Columbia clinical guideline on fall prevention, published for practitioners, outlines the kinds of factors clinicians typically review; reading it can help a patient describe relevant history clearly, but it does not replace an assessment.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

Why timing and framing change the conversation

A single near-stumble on uneven pavement is a different conversation than a fall that caused a head injury, loss of consciousness, new confusion, chest pain, or an inability to bear weight. The second group of symptoms warrants urgent or emergency care rather than waiting for a scheduled appointment with a mobile physiotherapist or physician. For non-urgent situations, deciding when to bring up a fall is still a real choice: some readers wait for a routine visit, others request a dedicated appointment because falls history affects program planning. Framing matters because a clinician will usually ask when the fall happened, what the reader was doing, whether it has happened before, and whether anything felt different afterward.

Preparing short, factual answers to those questions in advance, rather than waiting to recall details on the spot, tends to produce a clearer and more useful conversation. This article does not tell a reader whether a specific fall is urgent; it lays out the category distinction so the reader can apply it to their own situation or ask a clinician directly.

Using official prompts without self-scoring

BC's fall prevention guideline for practitioners lists categories such as prior falls, medication review, vision, footwear, home hazards, and balance or gait concerns that clinicians commonly consider during an assessment. Reading these categories can help a reader organize observations, for example noting that a fall happened on stairs at night, or that new medication started around the same time as increased unsteadiness. The guideline is written for practitioners, not as a public self-assessment tool, and this article does not convert it into a checklist that assigns a risk level or recommends exercises.

A reader who tries to self-score using the categories risks either false reassurance or unnecessary worry, because the guideline assumes clinical judgment applied to a full history and examination. The more useful approach is to bring the categories into the conversation as topics worth mentioning, letting the clinician decide what they mean. Readers can review the publicly posted guideline themselves and bring specific questions about terms they do not understand, rather than relying on a secondhand summary.

What to bring to the appointment

A short written account of the fall or near-fall, including the date, location, activity at the time, and anything unusual beforehand or afterward, helps a clinician far more than a general statement like feeling unsteady lately. Listing current medications, including over-the-counter products and supplements, is useful because some medications affect balance or blood pressure. Noting footwear worn at the time, lighting conditions, and whether a mobility aid was in use or available adds context without requiring the reader to draw conclusions about cause. If the reader has had more than one fall or near-fall in recent months, a simple dated list is more reliable than memory alone.

It is also reasonable to bring a list of questions, such as asking whether the clinician reviews categories similar to those in the provincial guideline, and what happens next if a concern is identified. None of this preparation is a substitute for describing symptoms accurately in real time, and readers should still mention anything that feels different, even if it does not fit neatly into a prepared list.

What this conversation does and does not decide

Raising a fall with a mobile physiotherapist, family physician, or another clinician starts a conversation; it does not by itself produce a program, a risk score, or a guarantee of any outcome. A clinician who hears about a fall may ask follow-up questions, suggest further assessment, refer to another professional, or determine that no immediate action is needed, and all of these are legitimate outcomes. Readers planning mobile physiotherapy or a healthy-aging program through a provider should understand that discussing a fall is a separate step from verifying that provider's licence or scope, and from deciding whether a particular program fits their situation.

This article does not recommend a specific program, exercise, or professional, and it does not state that any program reduces fall risk. If a reader wants to confirm a physical therapist's registration status before the appointment, that is a distinct verification step covered elsewhere in this guide. Treating the fall conversation, the provider verification, and the program decision as three separate questions tends to produce clearer outcomes than bundling them together.

Questions this guide answers

Should I use the fall-prevention guideline to score my own risk?

No, the guideline is written for practitioners performing a full assessment, not as a public self-scoring tool. Using it to assign yourself a risk level risks false reassurance or unnecessary worry. The more reliable use is to bring the categories it covers into a conversation with a clinician.

When is a fall urgent rather than something to mention at a routine visit?

A fall involving head injury, loss of consciousness, new confusion, chest pain, or inability to bear weight warrants urgent or emergency care rather than waiting. Falls without those features can often be discussed at a scheduled appointment, but readers uncertain about severity should seek prompt medical advice rather than guessing.

What should I bring to a clinician conversation about a fall?

A short written account of when and how the fall happened, a current medication list, and notes on footwear or lighting conditions at the time are useful. Bringing specific questions, rather than relying on memory alone, generally makes the conversation more productive.
