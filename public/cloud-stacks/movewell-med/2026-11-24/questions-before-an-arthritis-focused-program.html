Questions before an arthritis-focused program

A decision framework for evaluating an arthritis-focused mobility program, covering assessment, staffing, claims, and exit terms before enrolling.

Choosing an arthritis-focused program means deciding whether a specific offering matches a reader's actual needs, not whether arthritis itself can be treated by this guide. The decision point is: before committing time or money, what questions separate a program's marketing language from its operational reality? This framework organizes those questions into four areas — assessment and staffing, claims and evidence, cost and commitment, and coordination with existing care. It does not recommend any program, professional, or treatment, and it does not diagnose or score arthritis severity. It is intended to help a reader prepare informed questions for a conversation with a qualified professional.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

Who assesses you and what are their qualifications

An arthritis-focused program typically begins with some form of intake or assessment, and the first useful question is who performs it and what their professional background actually is. If a physical therapist is named, their registration can be checked through the CHCPBC public registry, and if a physician is named or described as leading the program, their standing can be checked through the CPSBC registrant directory. These checks confirm licence status only; they do not confirm that a specific professional is qualified for a specific arthritis presentation, nor that the program design has been reviewed by a regulator.

Ask directly who performs the initial assessment, whether that person is the same person who delivers ongoing sessions, and what happens if the assessor is not a regulated professional. Ask how staffing is structured across a multi-session program, since continuity and qualifications often change as a program progresses. A program that cannot clearly answer who assesses you, and with what credentials, has not given you enough information to evaluate it.

What claims does the program make and how are they supported

Arthritis programs sometimes use language like evidence-based, physician-led, or proven outcomes, and a careful reader should ask what specific evidence supports each claim rather than accepting the phrase itself. Section 9 of the Food and Drugs Act prohibits false or misleading advertising of drugs, which is relevant if a program bundles any product, supplement, or infusion into its arthritis offering; ask whether any product mentioned has a Drug Identification Number you can check yourself in Health Canada's Drug Product Database. Ask the program to name the specific source behind any claim about safety, effectiveness, or outcomes, and ask whether that source applies to arthritis generally or to the particular program being offered.

A program that says physician-led should be asked exactly what that physician does — reviews cases, sees every client, or lends a name to marketing — since these are different things. None of this tells a reader whether a program will help; it only tells them whether the program's own claims are specific and checkable.

What does the program cost and what are the exit terms

Cost questions for an arthritis-focused program should go beyond the headline price to cover the full financial commitment, because many programs bundle assessment fees, session packages, travel charges for mobile visits, and optional products or tests. Ask for an itemized breakdown: what is the assessment fee, what is included in each session, is there a minimum number of sessions required, and are there charges for missed or cancelled appointments. Ask whether fees are billed upfront as a package or per visit, since upfront packages can limit flexibility if a reader's needs change.

Equally important are exit terms: what happens if a reader wants to stop partway through a paid program, is any portion refundable, and is there a required notice period. Ask whether progress is reviewed at set intervals and what happens if the program is not meeting expectations at that review point. A program that discourages questions about stopping, or that makes exit difficult or costly, deserves extra scrutiny before enrollment.

How does the program coordinate with your existing care

An arthritis-focused program rarely operates in isolation, so a reader should ask how it coordinates with other professionals already involved in their care, such as a family physician, a rheumatologist, a pharmacist, or an existing physiotherapist. Ask whether the program will send assessment notes or progress reports to a reader's existing physician, and whether the reader must request this explicitly or whether it happens automatically with consent. Ask how the program handles a new or worsening symptom that falls outside its scope — does it have a defined process for referring a reader back to a physician or to urgent care.

Ask whether the program requires or recommends pausing or coordinating with any current medications, and if so, how that coordination happens and with whom. A program that cannot describe a clear communication pathway with a reader's other care providers may leave that reader to manage coordination alone, which is itself an important factor in deciding whether the program fits a reader's situation.

Questions this guide answers

Does checking a therapist's CHCPBC registration confirm they can treat arthritis effectively?

No. Registry verification confirms licence status only, not that a specific professional's approach is effective for a specific person's arthritis. Scope, consent, and program design are separate questions that still need to be asked directly of the program.

What should I ask if an arthritis program mentions an injectable or supplement?

Ask for the product's exact name and, if it is a drug, its Drug Identification Number, which can be checked in Health Canada's Drug Product Database. A listing there confirms authorization status only; it does not confirm the product is appropriate for your situation, and any marketing claim about it should be weighed against Section 9 advertising rules.

Is it reasonable to ask about refunds before starting an arthritis program?

Yes, asking about exit terms and refund conditions before paying for a multi-session package is a reasonable financial precaution. A program that cannot clearly describe its cancellation and exit policy has not given a reader enough information to make an informed commitment.
