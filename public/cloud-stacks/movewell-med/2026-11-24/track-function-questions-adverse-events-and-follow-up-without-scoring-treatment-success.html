Track function, questions, adverse events, and follow-up without scoring treatment success

Four simple tracking habits to record function, questions, side effects, and follow-up items between visits without creating an informal treatment score.

Once a mobile rehabilitation or healthy-aging program is underway, the central decision shifts from choosing a provider to keeping a useful record of what is happening. A plain log of function, questions, and anything unexpected gives a clinician better information than memory alone. This is not a scoring system, a recovery tracker, or a substitute for reporting urgent symptoms promptly. The four approaches below describe what to record, why, and how to keep the habit sustainable without turning a notebook into an unofficial diagnostic tool.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

1. Log daily or weekly function in plain, specific language

A function log works best when it describes concrete tasks rather than abstract feelings. Instead of writing 'better' or 'worse,' note whether a reader could climb a specific set of stairs, carry groceries from the car, or stand through a shower without needing to sit down. Dates and short entries matter more than detail; a single sentence after each visit or notable day is enough to show a pattern over weeks. This kind of log is useful to bring to a mobile physiotherapy or longevity program appointment because it gives the clinician something observable to discuss, rather than a vague impression formed under the stress of the appointment itself.

It is important that this remains descriptive, not evaluative — the reader is not assigning a grade to a treatment or deciding whether a program is 'working.' That judgment, including whether to continue, adjust, or stop a program, belongs to a conversation between the reader and their regulated provider, informed by the provider's own assessment methods.

2. Write down questions the moment they occur, not the night before an appointment

Questions are easiest to capture the moment they arise, because context and wording are usually lost by the next appointment. A running list — on paper or in a phone note — captures things like why a particular exercise was chosen, what a product name means, or why frequency changed from weekly to biweekly. Grouping questions loosely by topic (program logistics, a specific exercise, a product or supplement mentioned, billing) makes the list easier to work through efficiently during a time-limited visit. This habit also protects against a common problem: important questions about scope, consent, or cost get dropped when a reader is focused on describing symptoms.

Keeping the list separate from the function log means neither task crowds out the other. None of this is about verifying whether a specific answer is correct; readers who want to confirm a professional's registration can use the CHCPBC public registry or the CPSBC registrant directory, which are separate steps from tracking day-to-day questions.

3. Record anything unexpected — including possible adverse events — with date, description, and what happened next

An adverse-event note should capture what happened, when, and what the reader did about it, without trying to determine cause. Examples include new pain after an exercise session, dizziness during or after a home visit, a skin reaction near an IV site, or unexpected fatigue following a new activity. The entry does not need medical language; 'felt lightheaded for about ten minutes after standing up, sat down, resolved on its own' is complete and useful. If a symptom is severe, sudden, or matches a red-flag pattern — chest pain, sudden weakness, loss of consciousness, severe shortness of breath — the priority is contacting emergency services or a primary care provider immediately, not writing a log entry first.

For non-urgent events, bringing the written record to the next appointment lets the clinician ask informed follow-up questions and decide, within their own scope, whether any change to the program is appropriate.

4. Set explicit follow-up reminders instead of relying on memory

A follow-up list turns loose ends into trackable items with a date attached. After an appointment, a reader might note that a clinician said they would check on a referral, that a product name needs to be looked up, or that a reassessment was mentioned for six weeks out. Writing 'follow up by [date]: ask about referral status' is more reliable than trusting that the topic will resurface naturally. This list can also hold administrative items, such as confirming whether a program's fee structure changed or whether a cancellation policy applies to an upcoming visit.

Keeping follow-up items visible, perhaps on the same page as the question log, reduces the chance that something discussed once is never revisited. The goal across all four habits is the same: a clear, honest record that supports an informed conversation with a regulated provider, not an independent judgment about whether a program is succeeding.

Questions this guide answers

Is a function log the same as scoring whether a program is working?

No, a function log is a plain record of specific tasks and dates, not a scoring system. It is meant to support a conversation with a regulated clinician, who is better positioned to interpret whether changes relate to a program. Readers should avoid assigning numeric scores or deciding independently that a program has succeeded or failed.

What should I do if I notice something that might be an adverse event?

Write down the date, a plain description, and what happened afterward, and raise it with the relevant provider at the next reasonable opportunity. If the symptom is severe or sudden — such as chest pain, fainting, or severe shortness of breath — contact emergency services immediately rather than waiting to log it. A log entry is a follow-up tool, not a substitute for urgent care.

Do I need special software to track this information?

No, a notebook or a basic phone note is sufficient for function logs, questions, and follow-up reminders. The value comes from consistency and plain language, not from any particular format or tool. Some readers prefer separate sections for function, questions, and follow-up items so each stays easy to scan before an appointment.
