Questions before a longevity or healthy-aging program

A decision framework for evaluating a longevity or healthy-aging program before enrolling, covering staffing, claims, cost, and exit terms.

Choosing a longevity or healthy-aging program means deciding whether a specific offering matches a reader's actual goals, budget, and tolerance for unproven claims. These programs often combine assessment, coaching, and sometimes product or infusion components, so the decision is rarely one question but several linked ones. This framework organizes those questions into four groups: who delivers the program, what claims are made, what the program actually involves day to day, and what happens at the end. None of this substitutes for individual medical advice; it is a structure for gathering facts before a reader commits time or money.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

Who is actually delivering and supervising the program

A longevity or healthy-aging program's marketing may emphasize a named physician or medical lead, but that association does not confirm who performs each service a reader actually receives. A reasonable first question is which specific professional will conduct the intake assessment, which professional designs any physical component, and whether a physician reviews the plan personally or only in a general oversight capacity. Physical therapists practicing in British Columbia can be checked in the CHCPBC public registry, and physicians can be checked in the CPSBC registrant directory; both confirm registration status but not scope, consent practices, or appointment availability, which are separate questions a reader still needs to ask directly.

It is also worth asking whether the person conducting ongoing visits is the same person named in the program's marketing, since healthy-aging programs sometimes delegate recurring visits to other staff. Readers should request this in writing rather than relying on a verbal assurance, since staffing can change between the sales conversation and the first appointment.

What specific claims are being made about outcomes

Healthy-aging and longevity programs frequently use language like physician-led, evidence-based, or safe without specifying what evidence supports a particular claim for a particular reader. A useful exercise is to ask the program to name the exact study, guideline, or data source behind any outcome claim, rather than accepting the phrase itself as proof. Section 9 of the Food and Drugs Act restricts false or misleading advertising specifically for drugs, so if a program references a named product or infusion ingredient, a reader can reasonably ask for the product's authorization status rather than accepting a general wellness claim.

Health Canada's Drug Product Database can confirm whether a named drug product is authorized for sale in Canada, though checking the database does not mean a product is appropriate for any individual reader. If a program cannot or will not name its evidence source when asked directly, that gap itself is useful information for comparing it against alternatives, and it belongs in the reader's notes alongside other program details.

What the program actually involves on a weekly or monthly basis

Marketing materials for longevity programs often describe general goals like improved vitality or healthy aging without specifying the concrete schedule of visits, assessments, and check-ins a reader will experience. Before enrolling, it helps to ask how many sessions are included, how long each visit runs, whether sessions happen in person or remotely, and who the reader contacts between visits if a question or concern arises. It is also reasonable to ask what happens if the assigned provider is unavailable, whether a substitute follows the same plan, and how changes to the plan get documented and communicated back to the reader.

Some programs bundle physical assessment with nutrition or supplement recommendations; readers should ask whether those components are optional and how they are priced separately, since bundled pricing can obscure which specific service costs what. Keeping a written log of these answers, dated and attributed to a named staff member, gives a reader something concrete to compare against what is delivered once the program begins, rather than relying on memory of a sales conversation.

What the exit and dispute terms look like before signing anything

Every longevity or healthy-aging program should be able to answer, in writing, what happens if a reader wants to pause or cancel partway through a paid term. Reasonable questions include whether unused sessions are refundable, whether there is a minimum commitment period, and whether cancellation requires written notice or a specific number of days. Readers should also ask who to contact with a complaint about the program itself, as opposed to a complaint about an individual professional's conduct, since those may route to different places depending on whether the concern involves a regulated health profession, a product, privacy, or advertising content.

Programs that resist putting cancellation and refund terms in writing, or that only describe them verbally, present a practical risk independent of any clinical question. A reader who collects these answers before the first payment is in a stronger position if the program's actual delivery does not match what was initially described, and has a documented basis for any later dispute or regulator inquiry.

Questions this guide answers

Does checking a provider's registry status confirm the program itself is appropriate?

No, registry status only confirms that a named physical therapist or physician is currently registered to practice in British Columbia. It does not confirm program design, consent practices, staffing on a given visit, or whether a specific program suits a reader's situation, which are separate questions to ask the program directly.

What should a reader do if a longevity program won't specify its evidence source?

Ask directly for the exact study, guideline, or data source behind any outcome claim, and record whether the program can name one. If a program cannot provide a specific source when asked, that gap is useful information to weigh against other programs, though it does not by itself indicate wrongdoing.

Can the Drug Product Database tell a reader whether a product is right for them?

No, Health Canada's Drug Product Database confirms whether a named drug product is authorized for sale in Canada, nothing more. It does not select a treatment or establish that a product is appropriate for any individual reader's circumstances.
