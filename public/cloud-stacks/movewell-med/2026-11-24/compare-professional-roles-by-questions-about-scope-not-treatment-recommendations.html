Provider, scope, and preparation questionsPort Coquitlam, BCComparison

Compare professional roles by questions about scope, not treatment recommendations

Compare physiotherapists, physicians, and other providers by asking about scope of practice and licensing, not by seeking treatment recommendations from this guide.

Choosing between a physiotherapist, a physician, or another provider for a mobile rehabilitation or healthy-aging program is not about picking the most impressive title. It is about understanding what each professional is licensed and positioned to do, and what questions fall outside their role. This article does not recommend a profession or a program. Instead, it offers a framework of scope-based questions a reader can bring to any provider conversation, so the decision rests on verified scope and documented answers rather than assumptions about who handles what.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

Why scope questions differ from treatment questions

Asking a provider about their scope of practice is a different question than asking what treatment to choose. Scope questions address what a professional is licensed, trained, and authorized to assess or perform, such as whether a physical therapist can order imaging or whether a physician can prescribe medication. Treatment questions ask what should be done for a specific person's symptoms, which this guide does not answer. A reader comparing a mobile physiotherapy visit against a physician consultation benefits from separating these two categories before the appointment. For physical therapists, scope and standing can be checked through the College of Health and Care Professionals of BC public registry at chcpbc.org.

For physicians, the College of Physicians and Surgeons of BC maintains a registrant directory at cpsbc.ca/directory. Neither registry recommends a provider or confirms that a given service is appropriate; they confirm licence status and registration category, which is the starting point for any scope comparison.

Questions that reveal scope boundaries between roles

A useful comparison asks each professional the same set of boundary questions rather than asking for their opinion on a plan. Examples include: What can you assess directly, and what requires referral to another professional? Can you order diagnostic tests, and if not, who can? Are you able to prescribe or adjust medication, or does that require a physician? Do you work independently, or under a collaborative or supervisory arrangement with another regulated professional? For a mobile physiotherapy visit, a reader might ask whether the therapist operates within a physician-led practice model and what that collaboration covers versus what remains the therapist's independent scope.

For a physician consultation, a reader might ask whether a recommended program component, such as an IV service or a functional-medicine test, falls within standard medical practice or requires a separate referral. These questions surface real boundaries without asking either professional to recommend a specific path forward.

Using general professional regulation information as a starting point

BC's health professional regulatory framework, described by the Government of British Columbia at the professional-regulation resource, explains that different health professions are regulated by different colleges with distinct scopes of practice, standards, and complaint processes. This general information helps a reader understand why a physiotherapist and a physician are not interchangeable sources of the same answer, even when both are involved in the same program. A reader preparing to compare roles can review this material to understand that regulation exists at the profession level, not at the program or clinic level.

This matters when a mobile rehabilitation program advertises multiple categories, such as longevity, mobility-and-balance, arthritis, or IV services, since each category may involve different professionals with different scopes contributing to a single program. Confirming who is responsible for which part of a program, and checking that person's registration separately, is a more reliable comparison method than assuming one named medical lead oversees every detail of every service offered.

Turning scope comparisons into a documented conversation

The most practical outcome of comparing professional roles is a written list of questions and answers a reader can keep for later reference. Rather than relying on memory, a reader can note, for each professional involved in a prospective program, their registration category, what they confirmed they can assess or perform, what they said requires another provider, and any gaps that were not answered clearly. This record becomes useful if a reader later needs a second opinion, has a concern about a service, or wants to confirm that a program's described team matches what each individual professional confirmed about their own scope.

It also protects against assuming that a collaboration between a physician and other providers means every individual service has been separately reviewed, since a named collaboration does not verify every practitioner, service, or appointment. Keeping this documentation separate from any treatment decision keeps the comparison focused on scope and licensing rather than on recommending a course of action.

Questions this guide answers

Does asking about scope tell me which provider I should choose?

No, scope questions clarify what each professional is licensed to do, not which one is right for a particular situation. That decision depends on individual circumstances this guide cannot assess, so scope comparison is a preparation step, not a selection tool.

Can a physiotherapist do everything a physician can do in a mobile program?

No, physiotherapists and physicians have distinct scopes of practice regulated by separate colleges, so tasks like prescribing medication typically require a physician. Confirming each professional's registration category through the CHCPBC registry or CPSBC directory clarifies these boundaries before an appointment.

Does a physician-led program mean a physician personally reviews every service?

Not necessarily; a named medical lead or collaboration does not verify every practitioner, service, or appointment within a program. Readers can ask directly which specific services a physician reviews versus which are handled independently by other licensed professionals.
