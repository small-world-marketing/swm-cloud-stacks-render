Longevity ProgramPort Coquitlam, BCDecision notes

Record symptoms, function, history, medications, goals, and unanswered questions

A decision framework for organizing symptoms, function changes, history, medications, goals, and open questions before any mobility or longevity program appointment.

Before a first appointment with any mobile physiotherapist, physician, or healthy-aging program, the most useful preparation is a clear written record, not a self-diagnosis. This framework helps a reader organize what has changed, what has been tried, and what remains unclear, so a clinician can ask informed follow-up questions. It does not interpret symptoms, suggest a diagnosis, or recommend a treatment. It is meant to be filled in before a visit and brought along, whether the appointment is for a Longevity Program consultation, a Mobility & Balance Program, or an Arthritis Program inquiry.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

Describing symptoms and functional changes without self-diagnosing

A useful symptom record separates observation from interpretation. Instead of naming a suspected condition, note when a symptom started, what makes it better or worse, and how it has changed over weeks or months. For function, describe specific tasks: climbing stairs, carrying groceries, standing from a chair, or walking a certain distance, and whether these have become harder, easier, or inconsistent. Avoid assigning a cause, since that is a clinical judgment, not a reader's task. If a symptom is new, severe, or rapidly worsening, that detail belongs at the top of the record, since urgent or red-flag symptoms should be raised immediately rather than folded into routine program planning.

This section of the worksheet is also where a reader can note whether a symptom affects one side of the body, happens only during certain activities, or varies by time of day. Precise, dated observations are more useful to any physician or physiotherapist than a general impression, and they give the clinician something concrete to ask about during a limited appointment window.

Organizing medical history and current medications

A short, organized history helps any provider avoid redundant questions and reduces the chance of missing something relevant. List past surgeries, major diagnoses, hospitalizations, and ongoing conditions with approximate dates, rather than trying to summarize a full chart from memory. For medications, record the name, dose, and purpose as prescribed, including over-the-counter products and supplements, since these can matter for program eligibility or for how a provider interprets symptoms. This record does not require the reader to judge whether a medication is working or appropriate; that is a clinical question for the prescriber or pharmacist.

If a reader is unsure about a product's exact authorization status, Health Canada's Drug Product Database can be checked for the product's listed information, though this does not replace a conversation with a prescriber or establish that a product suits the reader. Keeping this list current before each appointment, including any recent changes, saves time and reduces the risk of conflicting information being given to different providers involved in a person's care.

Clarifying goals before discussing a program category

Goals should be specific enough to guide a conversation but should not be framed as a request for a guaranteed outcome. A goal such as wanting to walk to a specific location without stopping, or wanting to understand options for joint discomfort, gives a provider something concrete to respond to. A goal framed as wanting guaranteed pain relief, restored independence, or longevity is not something any program can promise, and no accurate provider conversation will offer that certainty. This record is also the place to distinguish between goals relevant to a Longevity Program, a Mobility & Balance Program, or an Arthritis Program, since each raises different questions about assessment, frequency, and realistic scope.

Writing goals down in advance also helps a reader notice if a goal shifts during the conversation, or if a provider's description of the program does not match what the reader actually wants to address, which is a useful signal to ask more questions before committing to anything.

Capturing unanswered questions for the next conversation

Every appointment tends to leave some questions open, and writing these down immediately afterward is more reliable than trying to remember them later. Useful entries include anything a provider said they would follow up on, any term that was not fully explained, and any discrepancy between what was discussed and what the reader expected. This is also where a reader can note if a claim was made using language like evidence-based, physician-led, or safe, since such claims can be checked against exact current evidence rather than accepted at face value.

Keeping a running list across multiple appointments helps when a reader is coordinating between a mobile physiotherapist, a physician, and possibly a pharmacist or specialist, since questions raised with one provider may need to be revisited with another. This record also supports continuity if the reader later seeks a second opinion, since having a dated list of unresolved questions makes that follow-up conversation faster and more precise than starting from memory alone.

Questions this guide answers

Should I write down a suspected diagnosis before my appointment?

No, a reader should record observations and functional changes rather than a suspected diagnosis, since diagnosis is a clinical judgment made by a qualified provider. Describing when a symptom started and what affects it gives a clinician useful information without assuming a cause. If a symptom is new or severe, it should be flagged as urgent rather than worked into routine program planning.

How should I record my medications before an appointment?

List each medication's name, dose, and purpose as prescribed, including supplements and over-the-counter products, so a provider has a complete picture. This record does not require judging whether a medication is working. Health Canada's Drug Product Database can confirm a product's listed authorization details, but it does not establish suitability for an individual reader.

What if my goals do not match what a program actually offers?

Writing goals down in advance makes it easier to notice a mismatch during the conversation, which is a useful reason to ask more questions before enrolling. No program can accurately promise guaranteed pain relief, independence, or longevity, so goals framed around those outcomes should be revisited with the provider directly.
