Questions before a mobility-and-balance program

A decision framework for Metro Vancouver readers weighing a mobility-and-balance program, covering assessment, staffing, scope, and exit terms to verify first.

Choosing a mobility-and-balance program means deciding whether a specific offering fits a reader's situation, not whether balance training works in general. This framework organizes the questions worth asking before enrolling: who assesses function, who delivers sessions, how progress is tracked, and what happens if the program does not fit. It does not score fall risk or recommend exercises. Readers in Port Coquitlam and nearby Metro Vancouver communities can use these prompts in a conversation with any provider, including programs a parent health brand such as Movewell Med may advertise, without assuming any single program is appropriate before confirming current details directly.

Editorial date November 24, 2026. This guide offers general information for planning conversations. Ask a qualified clinician about individual care.

Who performs the intake assessment and what does it cover

Before joining a mobility-and-balance program, a reader can reasonably ask who conducts the initial assessment and what it includes. An assessment performed by a physiotherapist differs from one performed by an unlicensed fitness coach, and the distinction matters because scope of practice shapes what can legally be evaluated and documented. Readers can ask whether the assessor is a physiotherapist and, if so, confirm current registration through the College of Health and Care Professionals of BC public registry, since licence status is a separate question from program design or availability. It is also worth asking what the assessment measures: gait, strength, balance confidence, medication review, or environmental factors, and whether findings are shared in writing.

A program that cannot describe its assessment process in concrete terms, or that treats assessment as a formality before enrollment, gives a reader less information to judge fit. Asking these questions does not require any clinical background; it simply clarifies who is responsible for the starting picture a program will later reference.

How is progress tracked without turning it into a success score

A mobility-and-balance program should be able to explain, in advance, what it tracks over time and how often. Reasonable items to ask about include session frequency, who delivers each session, whether the same provider sees the reader consistently, and how changes in function are recorded. Readers should be cautious of programs that frame tracking as a guaranteed improvement metric; tracking records observations over time, it does not establish that a program causes a particular outcome. A useful question is whether the program documents adverse events or near-falls during sessions, and how that information is communicated back to the reader or a family member.

Another is whether there is a defined review point, such as a four- or eight-week check-in, where continuation is reconsidered rather than assumed. Readers can also ask what happens if progress tracking shows no meaningful change: is the program adjusted, paused, or ended? Programs that cannot describe a review process, or that imply continued enrollment is the only path forward, leave readers with less ability to make an informed, time-limited decision.

What are the staffing, frequency, and duration terms in writing

Enrollment decisions depend heavily on operational details that are easy to overlook in an initial conversation. Readers should ask exactly who will be present at each visit, whether that person is a licensed physiotherapist or support staff working under supervision, and whether the same staffing applies for the full program duration. Frequency and duration matter because a twice-weekly six-week program creates different time and cost commitments than an open-ended monthly arrangement. It is reasonable to ask for these terms in writing, including what counts as a completed session, how rescheduling or cancellation is handled, and what the exit process looks like if a reader wants to stop early.

Readers should also ask whether eligibility criteria exist, such as a minimum assessment score or physician referral, and whether those criteria are checked before the first paid session. None of this requires evaluating clinical content; it is an operational verification step that applies to any mobility-and-balance program a reader is considering, regardless of which organization offers it.

How does this program fit alongside other care a reader already has

A mobility-and-balance program rarely exists in isolation from a reader's broader care picture, so it is worth asking how the program coordinates with existing providers. Relevant questions include whether the program requests information from a reader's family physician, whether it shares reports back, and whether it asks about current medications or other therapies already underway. Readers managing a chronic condition or recovering from a recent fall or surgery may want to confirm whether the program expects a physician's awareness before starting, even if a direct referral is not required. It is also fair to ask how the program handles a change in status, such as a new fall or a worsening symptom reported mid-program: does it pause sessions, suggest contacting a physician, or continue as scheduled?

Readers should treat any physician association named by a program as a separate question from whether a specific physiotherapist is currently registered, since a named medical lead does not verify every practitioner or session. Confirming coordination in advance helps a reader see the program as one piece of a larger care picture rather than a standalone fix.

Questions this guide answers

Does a mobility-and-balance program need to be led by a physiotherapist?

Not necessarily, but a reader can ask who performs the assessment and delivers sessions, and confirm any physiotherapist's current registration through the CHCPBC public registry. Programs may involve a mix of licensed and unlicensed staff, so asking who does what clarifies scope before enrolling.

What should be reviewed before deciding whether to continue a program?

Readers can ask for a defined review point, such as a scheduled check-in, where continuation, adjustment, or ending the program is discussed based on recorded observations rather than assumed automatically. This keeps the decision time-limited and revisited rather than open-ended.

Is a physician referral required for a mobility-and-balance program?

This varies by program, so it is a direct question to ask rather than an assumption. Even without a required referral, readers may want to confirm whether the program expects their family physician to be aware of participation.
