replacement window and exterior project decisions Chicagoland: Preparation Checklist 2

Use seven practical checks to prepare occupants, rooms, access, records, and older painted openings before exterior work begins.

Preparation should protect people, possessions, access, and decision records without shifting professional responsibilities onto the household. The immediate choice is whether the site is ready for the agreed work to begin. Readiness means more than moving a chair. It includes confirming the exact openings, identifying vulnerable occupants, resolving access constraints, documenting existing conditions, and understanding how dust and debris will be controlled. Homes built before 1978 require particular attention when painted components may be disturbed. Consumers should ask about federal lead-safe requirements and verify the firm’s status rather than attempting hazardous disturbance themselves. Use these seven checks at the pre-start meeting, and postpone work when a material question remains unanswered.

Seven checks before the crew arrives

1. Match the schedule to the written scope. Walk through every opening and exterior area with the current proposal. Use room names or numbered labels, not memory. Confirm product configuration, color, operation, glass or panel choice, hardware, trim treatment, and work side. Photograph labels after delivery when practical, but do not open packaging or direct installation. Establish who can approve a substitution and require written authorization before one occurs. 2. Document existing conditions. Take date-stamped photographs of walls, floors, landscaping, window treatments, furniture, alarms, siding, trim, and each work area. Note existing cracks, stains, difficult operation, or damaged finishes without deciding their cause. Share disputed observations before mobilization. Documentation supports a fair completion review; it does not prove responsibility by itself. 3. Create safe access. Ask the provider for required interior clearance, exterior staging area, parking, electrical access, and pathway width. Let the crew specify what must be moved. Arrange professional help for heavy furniture, hardwired treatments, security devices, or valuable fixtures. Discuss pets, gates, children, mobility needs, home offices, and any resident who cannot tolerate an unsecured opening. Never assume a room remains usable during removal. 4. Review containment and cleanup. Ask how floors, furnishings, landscaping, and adjacent finishes will be protected; where debris goes; how pathways remain controlled; and who performs daily and final cleanup. Clarify whether equipment may block entrances or shared areas. For multifamily property, confirm authorized work hours and building coordination separately. 5. Address painted components in older homes. If the home or affected component predates 1978, ask whether the work will disturb paint and how the EPA Renovation, Repair and Painting requirements apply. EPA consumer guidance explains certified-firm and lead-safe-practice questions. Verify certification using current official resources and request required consumer information. Do not sand, scrape, drill, test by disturbance, or remove painted parts merely to help the project begin. 6. Plan temporary conditions. Ask how openings are protected if weather interrupts work, how interior temperature is managed, which entrances remain available, and who responds if coverings shift. Set a daily closing check covering locks, temporary seals, debris, tools, and pet barriers. A plan is not a guarantee of weather, timing, or security; it assigns actions and contacts. 7. Prepare the decision file. Keep the signed agreement, current scope, product schedule, drawings, color approvals, notices, insurance information supplied by the contractor, payment record, and contact list together. Establish one household representative and one provider contact. Decide how discoveries, photographs, changes, and approvals will be recorded.

Use a stoplight review at the pre-start meeting

Mark green only when the item is written, understood, and assigned. Use yellow for a manageable unknown with a named decision point, such as concealed substrate that can be evaluated only after removal. Mark red when work should not begin: the delivered configuration conflicts with the order, an affected opening is absent from the scope, lead-related questions remain unresolved, critical access has not been arranged, or no authorization process exists for discovered conditions. For every yellow item, record who examines it, what evidence will be shared, whether work pauses, and who may approve price or scope changes. A verbal assurance that the team will decide later is not a control. For every red item, state the correction needed and who confirms closure. Update the planned start only after the necessary record exists. Discuss occupants as individuals rather than treating the property as empty workspace. A resident working overnight, an infant, a person with respiratory sensitivity, or someone dependent on a particular doorway may require sequencing or temporary relocation decisions. Obtain appropriate medical guidance for personal health concerns; a contractor should not diagnose exposure risk. Finish with a room-by-room readiness walk. Confirm that only agreed belongings have been moved, coverings and containment will be installed by the responsible party, and emergency routes remain understood. Exchange day-of contacts and define the morning go/no-go decision when severe weather or an unexpected site condition arises. Preparation succeeds when the crew can perform the written scope while residents understand boundaries, protections, and escalation steps.

Questions this guide answers

What should residents move before window work?

Ask the provider for opening-specific clearance and protection requirements. Arrange qualified help for heavy, valuable, hardwired, or security-related items rather than moving them impulsively.

What should owners of pre-1978 homes ask?

Ask whether painted material will be disturbed, how federal RRP requirements apply, whether the firm is currently certified, and what consumer information and containment plan will be provided.

When should a start be postponed?

Pause when products or scope do not match, essential safety or access arrangements are missing, lead-related obligations are unresolved, or no written change process exists.
