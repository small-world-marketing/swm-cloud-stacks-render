replacement window and exterior project decisions Chicagoland: Cost and Scope Questions 2

Turn unlike window, door, and exterior estimates into comparable scopes without treating the lowest total as the complete project cost.

Project totals are difficult to compare when proposals describe different work. One may include removal to the rough opening, another may preserve the existing frame, and a third may leave trim, painting, disposal, or concealed repairs undecided. The headline price therefore answers little until the scope boundaries are normalized. Your decision is not merely which estimate is lower; it is which defined package best matches the diagnosed condition and your tolerance for unresolved cost. Build a comparison sheet that separates product, installation, adjacent work, allowances, exclusions, and future responsibilities. This method does not predict final price or eliminate surprises. It reveals where each proposal assigns uncertainty and provides a disciplined basis for follow-up questions.

Normalize the base scope before comparing totals

Begin with one row per opening or exterior area. Record location, approximate dimensions if supplied, operating type, removal approach, named product configuration, finish colors, glass or panel options, hardware, screens, trim treatment, and disposal. Do not substitute a brand family for a model or configuration. Product ratings vary, so the supporting documents should identify the exact unit and relevant values rather than inviting conclusions from frame material, pane count, or advertising terms. Next describe the installation boundary. Does the proposal cover the complete existing assembly, an insert within a retained frame, or another method? Which parts remain? Who evaluates retained wood or substrate? What preparation occurs before the new unit is set? Ask how the opening will shed water and how new flashing, sealants, trim, and cladding connect. DOE installation guidance provides useful vocabulary for discussing assessment and water management, but the contractor must describe the proposed detail for the actual wall and opening. Create separate rows for interior casing, drywall or plaster repair, staining, painting, exterior trim, siding disturbance, threshold or sill work, electrical or alarm components, furniture moving, coverings, cleanup, and waste removal. Mark included, excluded, allowance, unit price, or unknown. An exclusion is not necessarily objectionable if you understand who will complete it, when it must occur, and what it may do to the schedule. Finally, align quantities. A proposal covering more openings, upgraded hardware, or extensive finish restoration should not be compared directly with a narrower offer. Calculate no invented per-opening benchmark; instead, reconcile the scope first. A higher total may contain more work, while a lower one may appropriately reflect a limited repair. The document should make the difference visible.

Assign uncertainty through allowances and change rules

Concealed deterioration is a common source of uncertainty because it may not be visible before removal. Ask each provider to state what inspection has already occurred, what conditions remain concealed, and what happens if damaged framing, substrate, flashing, or finishes appear. Useful terms include a unit price for defined repair, a capped allowance, or a written change-order process. Avoid treating a vague promise to handle whatever appears as a complete cost plan. For every allowance, identify the assumed quantity, quality level, labor inclusion, tax treatment, markup method, and reconciliation process. Ask whether unused funds are credited and whether work stops for written authorization before an overage. For owner-selected products, note selection deadlines and consequences of late choices. For exclusions, obtain a realistic coordination point rather than assuming another trade can appear immediately. Illinois consumer materials should be reviewed before signing. The Illinois Attorney General provides home-repair guidance, while the Illinois Home Repair and Remodeling Act contains current statutory text. Use those official resources to prepare questions about written agreements, notices, payment terms, and changes; obtain qualified legal advice for interpretation of a particular dispute or contract. This article is an organizational framework, not a legal conclusion about any transaction. Compare payment schedules by milestone and evidence, not percentage alone. Define what makes a milestone complete: delivery is different from installation, and installation is different from accepted finish work or closed punch-list items. Record who holds warranties, permits if applicable, product labels, photographs, invoices, and change approvals. The best-evidenced proposal is the one whose total can be traced to a specific configuration and work boundary. Before selecting it, produce a one-page reconciliation of meaningful differences, remaining unknowns, and potential owner-paid tasks. If the comparison still depends on verbal explanations, ask for the proposal to be revised. A clear scope is part of the purchase, not paperwork to reconstruct afterward.

Questions this guide answers

Why can two estimates for the same openings differ substantially?

They may use different products, removal methods, installation boundaries, finish work, allowances, quantities, or exclusions. Normalize those elements before interpreting the totals.

How should concealed damage appear in a proposal?

The document should identify what remains unknown and provide a defined inspection, authorization, pricing, and documentation process if hidden conditions are discovered.

Should verbal inclusions count during comparison?

Treat them as unresolved until they are incorporated into the written scope with enough detail to identify the work, materials, price treatment, and responsibility.
