replacement window and exterior project decisions Chicagoland: Cost and Scope Questions

A scope-first checklist for comparing window and exterior estimates, allowances, exclusions, change orders, payment terms, and concealed conditions.

Two estimates with similar totals can describe materially different projects, while proposals with different prices may not be directly comparable. The useful decision is therefore not simply which number is lower. It is whether each number covers the same openings, removal method, materials, water-management work, finishes, cleanup, and risk allocation. Start with an opening schedule: give every window or door an identifier, room, approximate configuration, observed problem, and intended action. Require each bidder to use that schedule or provide an equally precise substitute. This prevents a count from concealing differences such as a fixed unit versus an operable one, an insert versus fuller removal, or unfinished interior trim versus completed finishing. Prices change with site conditions and selections, so this checklist avoids invented local ranges and concentrates on what the homeowner can verify in writing.

Define what the base amount buys

For each opening, confirm the manufacturer, series, model or configuration, operation, size basis, frame and exterior color, interior finish, glazing package, hardware, screens, grilles, and accessibility features where relevant. Ask which selections are final and which are allowances. A product description such as “double-pane vinyl window” is too broad for meaningful comparison because it does not identify documented ratings, options, or compatibility with the opening. Describe removal and installation separately. Does the proposal retain the existing frame, remove it to the rough opening, or use another method? Which stops, trim, cladding, sill components, or siding pieces will be removed? Who replaces deteriorated wood discovered within the agreed exposure? The DOE installation job aid offers a useful sequence for questioning sill preparation, flashing, shimming, fastening, insulation or air sealing, and exterior water control. A contract need not copy that document, but the provider should explain the project-specific method and materials. List interior and exterior completion work. Include jamb extensions, casing, stool or apron work, paint-ready versus painted surfaces, exterior trim or capping, sealants, siding transitions, threshold finishing, debris removal, and protection of nearby landscaping or finishes. Clarify whether disposal includes old glass, frames, packaging, and any separately regulated material. If electrical, alarm, shade, masonry, or carpentry work is excluded, identify who coordinates it and when. Treat access equipment and logistics as real scope. Upper-story openings, constrained side yards, attached structures, large units, parking limitations, and interior furniture protection can affect the approach. Ask bidders to state assumptions rather than leaving them implicit. The goal is not to eliminate every uncertainty; it is to expose the assumptions that could later alter the amount.

Allocate uncertainty before signing

Create a table with rows for product, removal, opening repair, flashing, air sealing, finishes, disposal, access, permits, lead-related procedures, schedule, payment, and warranties. Mark each proposal as included, excluded, allowance, or unclear. Resolve every unclear item in writing. Avoid filling gaps with verbal impressions. Concealed conditions deserve a defined process. Ask what evidence triggers additional work, who documents the finding, whether work pauses, how labor and materials are priced, and who must approve a change. A change-order clause should state that modifications are written and accepted before the extra work proceeds, except for a genuine immediate safety issue addressed by the contract. Photographs can preserve what was exposed and why the scope changed. Review deposits and progress payments against Illinois home-repair guidance and the applicable written terms. Understand the total or pricing formula, due dates, financing conditions, cancellation provisions where applicable, and what constitutes completion. Do not interpret a large deposit, small deposit, or particular payment schedule as proof of legitimacy by itself. Verify the contractor’s identity and relevant credentials through their issuers, and do not pay for an undocumented scope. Finally, separate three categories in the budget: contracted base work, homeowner-elected upgrades, and contingency for genuinely concealed conditions. Keep optional enhancements—special finishes, upgraded hardware, alternative glass, or additional openings—visible rather than blending them into necessary remediation. A fair comparison emerges when the same decision sits in the same column. The winning proposal is the one whose documented scope and risk allocation suit the house and owner’s priorities, not automatically the cheapest or most extensive package.

Questions this guide answers

How can homeowners compare estimates with different totals?

Normalize them by opening and mark product details, removal, installation, finishing, exclusions, allowances, disposal, payment terms, and warranties in the same table.

What should happen when concealed damage is found?

The agreement should define documentation, pricing, approval, and whether work pauses before an authorized written change proceeds.
