integrative cancer care questions and evidence review Wisconsin: Maintenance Decisions

A records-based guide for reassessing ongoing complementary services when health, treatment, evidence, or costs change.

An initial decision does not settle whether an ongoing complementary service should continue unchanged. Cancer treatment evolves, medicines are added, symptoms appear, costs accumulate, and commercial protocols may be revised. Maintenance therefore means periodic reassessment, not automatic renewal. This guide was reviewed August 24, 2026 and provides general information rather than medical advice or a judgment about any service. Do not independently continue, pause, restart, or modify oncology treatment. Discuss ongoing complementary products and procedures with the oncology team, especially after clinical changes, because earlier coordination advice may no longer fit the present situation.

Run a structured review at meaningful changes

Set reassessment points around events, not a generic promise to “check later.” Relevant triggers include a new cancer-treatment plan, surgery, hospitalization, infection, changed laboratory results, new prescription, altered organ function, pregnancy, new allergy, worsening symptom, adverse event, or change in the complementary protocol. A new ingredient, device, route, frequency, practitioner, or purpose creates a new proposal even when the service retains the same name. Reconfirm the objective. Ask what outcome is being pursued now and how it will be observed. Keep symptom relief, function, general wellness, laboratory measures, and cancer outcomes distinct. If the goal has drifted from comfort to treatment of disease, require evidence addressing that different question. Do not reinterpret ordinary variation or a single measurement as proof of benefit. Update the complete care inventory. Reconcile prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, injections, infusions, manual practices, devices, diets, and procedures. Send changes to the oncology pharmacist or other designated clinician. Record actual dates and amounts when known. Missing use should be marked unknown rather than reconstructed from memory. Review the evidence source again. Determine whether the cited research still matches the formulation, equipment, patient group, comparison, and outcome. Separate newly published human evidence from laboratory announcements or marketing summaries. Government guidance can explain concepts and evidence boundaries, but it does not make an individualized decision. Revisit consent and cost. Ask whether risks, alternatives, uncertainty, emergency arrangements, privacy terms, price, package duration, cancellation provisions, or refund rules changed. Continuing payment is not continuing informed consent. A renewal discount should not replace clinical reassessment.

Maintain a useful record without interpreting causation

Keep a chronological encounter log. For each service, record date, stated purpose, products or device used, responsible practitioner, measurements taken, instructions received, and records transmitted. Attach receipts and updated consent forms. This creates continuity when professionals change or when the oncology team needs to reconstruct exposure. Maintain a separate symptom log using neutral observations: start time, duration, location, severity in the patient’s own consistent scale, associated events, and contacts made. Do not label a symptom a “healing reaction,” detoxification, treatment success, or definite side effect without clinical assessment. Follow oncology triage instructions for concerning changes and emergency guidance for urgent conditions. For ozone or EBOO-related commercial claims, maintenance review must not become a how-to procedure. Ask for the exact device, process description, oversight, monitoring, record pathway, and adverse-event reporting plan. The federal ozone provision is a regulatory safety and labeling boundary, not evidence that a recurring treatment is beneficial. Repeated uneventful sessions do not establish safety for future sessions or cancer effectiveness. Check professional responsibilities periodically. Verify regulated Wisconsin credentials in the DSPS lookup when a responsible person changes. Confirm who now orders, supervises, monitors, and handles complications. Credential status and public discipline information help answer accountability questions, while the clinical claim still requires separate evidence. Use four possible review outcomes without ranking treatments: continue discussion under the oncology team’s documented plan; request missing information; pause the commercial service pending qualified review; or decline further sessions. The appropriate choice depends on current clinical advice, evidence, consent, and individual circumstances. Preserve unresolved questions rather than forcing certainty. At closeout, request final notes, administered-product details, device information, measurements, adverse-event documentation, and follow-up instructions. Cancel recurring charges through the written process and retain confirmation. Keep the resulting packet with oncology records so future clinicians can see what occurred.

Questions this guide answers

When should an ongoing service be reassessed?

Reassess after clinical, medication, evidence, protocol, practitioner, cost, consent, or symptom changes.

Does having no problem during earlier sessions prove future safety?

No. Prior experience cannot establish safety under changed health conditions, treatment plans, products, or procedures.
