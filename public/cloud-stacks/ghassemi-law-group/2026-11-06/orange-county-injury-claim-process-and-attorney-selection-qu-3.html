Deciding what to share before a first appointment

Decide what information a verified office needs for booking, what belongs in a private consultation and how to confirm the next step.

When booking an appointment, a person may be asked for enough information to identify the subject of an inquiry. That is different from supplying everything a lawyer might eventually need to review a matter. Decide what to share by identifying the purpose of the current step, the person receiving the information, and the channel available. A useful booking conversation can be quite short even when the underlying file is extensive.

Start with the question the office needs to route

Prepare a neutral description that identifies the kind of incident, its general location and the reason you are calling. An example for practice might be, “I have a letter about a vehicle incident in Irvine and need to ask a lawyer about the document.” That tells an office which kind of discussion is requested. It leaves room for the office to explain its intake process, identify possible conflicts, and tell you what information is needed next.

Ask who is speaking and what they can do at this stage. Scheduling staff may arrange a conversation and identify materials requested by a lawyer. A lawyer can answer questions within an appropriate professional relationship. If you are concerned about a printed response date or another urgent matter, mention the concern promptly. Ask how it can reach someone qualified to address it. Waiting to prepare a perfect summary can be counterproductive when timing matters.

Set a boundary around each transmission

Suppose the office asks for a picture of the first page of a letter so it can identify the sender. Examine what else appears on that page. It may contain a policy number, a home address or reference to another family member. Ask whether an index or a limited copy is sufficient initially and how the office wants the full document supplied if a lawyer needs it. Do not alter an original. Clearly identify any limited or redacted working copy.

A different decision arises when an office asks for a signed form, a recorded account or access to a larger file. Ask what the request authorizes and who is qualified to explain its consequences. A person can ask to see the complete document and preserve the wording they received. This guide cannot determine whether a particular request is necessary or how someone should respond. A licensed California lawyer reviewing the circumstances can address the question.

Make the appointment confirmation an accurate handoff

Write down the name and role of the scheduled participant, the date and time zone, any stated charge, and the method of meeting. Keep the office's contact details separately from a link in an unfamiliar message. The State Bar of California offers a public attorney-search resource that can help match a lawyer's identity to the available public record. Ghassemi Law Group's own website is a source for the firm's publicly stated information when it is the office you want to reach.

At the end of the call, read back what is expected: the scheduled conversation, the exact items requested and the route by which you will receive confirmation. If a follow-up is promised but has not arrived, avoid treating it as completed. Your note can simply say “instructions expected” with the date and contact. It is easier to correct an incomplete handoff when the missing step is visible.

If the caller is booking for someone else

Make the relationship and the limited purpose of the call clear at the start. Ask how the person who needs the appointment can confirm their preference or contact the office directly. An office may be able to explain its public booking process without discussing an individual's private matter. Be ready to separate those two conversations. For example, a relative can ask whether a video appointment is available without giving a detailed account of another person's injury. If the individual wants the relative involved later, ask the lawyer how participation and authority will be handled for that meeting.

Questions this guide answers

What if the person answering is a scheduler?

Ask which process questions they can answer and how an urgent legal question reaches a qualified professional.

Can a relative ask about appointment logistics?

A relative can ask about public booking arrangements. Ask the office how the individual should confirm participation or authorize access.
