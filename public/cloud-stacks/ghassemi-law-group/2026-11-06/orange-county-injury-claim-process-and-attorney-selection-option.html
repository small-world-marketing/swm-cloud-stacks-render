Comparing two written proposals for the same legal work

Compare two proposed legal engagements by matching each task to a written term and identifying the points that still need an explanation.

Two proposals can show a similar fee arrangement while offering different work. One may describe communication with an insurer. Another may also discuss filing papers or handling later proceedings. The useful comparison begins with the job you actually want explained. Write one neutral sentence describing that job and give each prospective office the same version. A comparison based on consistent inputs reveals differences that an advertising slogan or a single number can hide.

Compare the same event in each proposal

Consider a fictional driver who has a property estimate, a medical billing question and a letter asking for documents. The driver wants to understand which of those subjects an engagement would cover. On a sheet of paper, make a column for each proposal. Across the left margin write the three tasks in the driver's own words. In each box put a quotation or paragraph reference from the proposed agreement, followed by a short question when the document is silent. An empty box is a request for clarification. Avoid filling it with an assumption about what an office usually handles.

Work through one event at a time. Suppose the person who sent the letter asks for a response on a different subject. Which proposal explains how a new subject would be evaluated? Who would contact the client? Would the agreement need revision? The answers may be easier to compare when everyone is looking at the same event. When a lawyer says a question depends on the facts, record which missing fact matters and who is in a position to supply it.

Find the handoff points

A reader can learn a great deal by marking every line that transfers work to somebody else. A document might mention a referring lawyer, a records service, an outside expert or another office handling a separate issue. Ask which participant the client would contact, what information would be shared, and where approval for a change would appear. Do not infer the authority of any person from a business card or a friendly introduction. Ask the prospective lawyer to explain the specific role.

Include routine work in the comparison. How would the client update contact details? Who handles a correction to a chronology? How are important papers delivered when the client cannot use an online portal? One office might describe its process clearly while another leaves the point open for discussion. Record that difference as “written explanation received” or “question outstanding.” Those descriptions are more defensible than assigning a score for responsiveness from a single conversation.

Make room for information that cannot be compared yet

Select a date to review your notes. At that point, a blank should have an owner and a next step. For example, “Office B will send the expense exhibit mentioned on page two” is actionable. “Office B is more expensive” is a conclusion the available material may not support. If revised papers arrive, retain the earlier proposals and identify which version is under consideration. A material change in one column can affect comparisons elsewhere on the sheet.

Finally, confirm the full name of the person who would perform legal work and check the public attorney profile through the State Bar of California. Its consumer material also offers guidance on selecting counsel. Verification is one fact to record alongside the proposed scope. It cannot determine whether a particular engagement suits a person's needs. Ask a qualified California lawyer about the legal effect of actual terms, especially where a document limits work or describes what happens if representation ends.

A practical way to compare revisions

Use a separate color or symbol for answers that exist only in a conversation. On the day you receive a revised agreement, look for one of those answers in the writing. If the point was where to send a corrected photograph, identify the actual contact process described. If the point was representation at a later stage, ask which words describe that stage. This modest exercise prevents a useful conversation from becoming an assumed contract term when you return to the papers a week later.

Questions this guide answers

How do I compare different service descriptions?

Give both offices the same example and ask which tasks the proposed writing covers. Record a paragraph reference or an outstanding question.

What should a blank comparison box mean?

Label it as unanswered and identify who can clarify it. A missing explanation alone does not establish a particular price or service.
