From the publisher

A claim number scribbled on a repair estimate can mean three different things to the next person who reads it. It might be the insurer's file number, the repair shop's work order, or a number copied from a police card. After an Irvine injury incident, a small, clearly labeled packet can spare a family member a morning of calls. It can also give a California lawyer a clearer starting point for a consultation. The goal is to show what happened to each document, which facts are firsthand, and which questions still need an answer.

A tidy packet cannot decide who was at fault, what insurance covers, or what a claim is worth.

Make one page that points to the rest

Start with a sheet called “Where to find things.” Put the incident date, the approximate location, and the name of the person maintaining the packet at the top. A time or street name can be uncertain. Write “about 5 p.m., from my own recollection” or “intersection shown on the receipt” so that a later reader can see the basis for an entry. A time printed on a photograph, a time on an invoice, and the time someone remembers hearing a collision can all belong in the same packet without resolving the differences immediately.

Give each item a short identifier. For example, PHOTO-01 can refer to the original image of a bicycle wheel, SHOP-01 to a written repair estimate, and CALL-01 to a note about a conversation with the insurer. Put those labels in the index and on working copies. Keep original files, full email messages, and envelopes as received. If someone later supplies a clearer scan, add it as another copy and record who supplied it. When a working copy has someone else's phone number covered for sharing, label it as a redacted copy so it cannot be mistaken for the original.

Use the recipient's name to distinguish reports

Consider a fictional Irvine driver who receives a police incident card, an insurer's email, and a California DMV confirmation. Three numbers appear next to the same incident date. Create one entry for each recipient. Put the police department and the exact label printed on the card beside the first entry. Put the insurer, the policyholder's role, and the email subject beside the second. Record the DMV document's own title beside the third. Store each confirmation with the outgoing document it belongs to. A draft form alone does not show what the recipient received.

California DMV describes its SR-1 filing separately from reports to police and an insurer. Its public page identifies the information needed for its process. A packet can point the reader to that page without publishing a driver's licence number or vehicle identifier in a shared index. The California Department of Insurance also maintains a consumer guide for automobile accidents. Use the current agency guidance for its own process. A fact-specific question about legal timing or responsibility belongs with a licensed California lawyer.

Give the next reader a useful place to begin

Finish by listing two or three questions in ordinary language. “Does this letter refer to the first estimate or the revision?” is easier to act on than “insurance problem.” Add the document identifier beside each question. If an item has a printed response date, record who printed it and where it appears. Keep it distinct from any legal filing deadline. A person concerned about timing can bring the original to a qualified professional promptly, even if several items in the packet are missing.

Before sending records, confirm the recipient and ask which files are useful for the purpose at hand. An initial booking conversation may need only a short description. A lawyer can specify a secure way to provide a larger packet and explain the next step in that office's process. The State Bar of California's public search can help readers match a lawyer's full name with a licence record. For readers looking for the firm's own description of its services, the official Ghassemi Law Group website is a separate reference.

The packet is ready for handoff when another authorized reader can find one original, identify its source, and tell you the next open question without hearing the whole story again. If that test fails, improve the index or the label. More pages rarely solve the problem of an unidentified number.

Questions this publication answers

What belongs in a shared index?

Use short identifiers and locations that an authorized person needs. Keep sensitive identifiers and private records in a separate, appropriately protected place.

How should an approximate time be recorded?

State that it is approximate, name its source, and keep it distinct from the time shown on an independently issued record.
