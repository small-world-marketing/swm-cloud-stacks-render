Paper originals, working scans and family access

Compare paper-first, scan-first and divided filing arrangements using a two-sided original, an electronic copy and a household access test.

A safe place for a paper original is not necessarily a good place for a family member's working notes. A scan that is easy to find may omit the reverse side of a signed page. Choose a storage arrangement by asking what each reader needs to do. The person who keeps originals, the person who schedules appointments, and the professional who reviews a document may need different copies and different access.

Compare three workable arrangements

A paper-first arrangement can suit a small file containing a few original letters or receipts. Number the sleeves rather than marking an original. Put an index at the front and record when an item temporarily leaves the file. Its advantage is the ability to inspect a complete physical item, including an envelope or handwritten note. Its limitation is that two people in different places cannot inspect the same original at once.

A scan-first arrangement can make a long correspondence trail easier to find. Give each scan a meaningful identifier and retain the original's location in the index. Check both sides, all attachments and the final page. Open the saved file and check that small print is readable at a normal zoom. The existence of a PDF is no assurance that every page was captured. A missing reverse side can be obvious with the original in hand and invisible to a remote reviewer.

A divided arrangement keeps the originals in one protected place, selected working copies in another, and a limited shared index in a third. This can be useful where a relative helps with appointments but should have no general access to medical or financial material. It requires discipline. If an updated file is shared without updating the index, readers can end up relying on a copy whose relationship to the original is unclear.

Use a small test before reorganizing a large file

Choose a two-sided letter and its envelope. Give the letter a source and date label; give the envelope a related identifier. Make a working scan and check whether the association survives if someone downloads only the PDF. Then try to locate the physical original starting from the electronic index. If you must remember a drawer or guess at a filename, add an explicit locator that is meaningful to an authorized person.

Now reverse the test. Starting from the physical item, find the latest working copy and identify any annotations made after scanning. A note added by a family member should remain attributable to that person. If the note contains a correction or recollection, date it and preserve its source. A later reader should be able to tell an organization-issued document from an observation added in the household.

Decide how a handoff will end

When someone stops helping, review their access with them. Identify working copies held on a shared device, any originals borrowed for an appointment, and any records transferred to an authorized professional. Ask the responsible person about retention and return requirements for the actual documents. Do not select a destruction date simply because a folder is becoming inconvenient.

The practical choice is the arrangement the household can maintain consistently. Keeping a smaller, accurate index may be preferable to creating elaborate folders nobody updates. Sensitive records warrant appropriate storage and an agreed transfer method. Where legal retention, ownership, privacy or disclosure questions arise, get advice specific to the file. A storage system should support the decision of an authorized person, not quietly make the decision for them.

Label a temporary loan

An original statement is taken to an appointment and a photocopy stays in the household file. Place a simple locator slip in the original's sleeve naming the item, the person who took it and the date. If the appointment ends with the original still at the office, update the slip from the actual receipt or verified conversation. When the original comes back, reconcile the entry. This small paper trail can save an unnecessary request for a replacement.

Questions this guide answers

How can I check whether a scan is usable?

Open it, count the pages, inspect both sides and attachments, and confirm that an authorized person can locate the physical original.

What is a locator slip for?

It records where an original went, who took it and how to follow up, while the working copy stays in the household file.
