employee workplace rights Pennsylvania and New Jersey: Preparation Checklist 2

Build a consultation-ready workplace file through eight practical checks covering chronology, evidence, jurisdiction, objectives, and secure sharing.

Preparation is not the same as proving a case. Its purpose is to make a workplace question understandable to an agency, adviser, union representative, or other authorized reviewer. The eight checks below produce a compact file while preserving uncertainty and document integrity. They work for concerns involving termination, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, leave, disability, wages, severance, or restrictive agreements, although different topics need different records. Reviewed September 1, 2026. This checklist is informational, not legal advice. Recheck agency procedures and all time-sensitive propositions before publication and before each monthly reuse.

Checks 1–3: frame the event and its setting

1. Write a neutral issue sentence. Describe the decision or event without announcing that a law was violated. “My schedule changed after I sent a written complaint” is more useful than a conclusion because it identifies two events that can be verified. Add the question now facing the employee: whether to report, respond, sign, seek leave information, contact an agency, or request professional review. 2. Build a date-and-source chronology. Use one row per event with date, time if known, participants, location, what happened, supporting item, and confidence level. Mark estimates as estimates. Distinguish a message read contemporaneously from a later recollection. Include favorable, unfavorable, and ambiguous facts; a selective timeline is harder to evaluate. Record the employer's stated explanation exactly and identify where it appears. Do not silently correct or improve quotations. 3. Create a jurisdiction card. Note the work location for relevant events, employer location, remote-work arrangement, approximate workforce information if actually known, union or public-employment status, and any agreement naming a forum or governing law. These facts may influence which process applies, but the card should not decide coverage. Pennsylvania's PHRC and New Jersey's Division on Civil Rights are different state institutions; the EEOC concerns federal employment-discrimination law where applicable. Preserve uncertainty when work crossed state lines. The card should also list every known pending date and its source: an employer email, agency notice, policy, agreement, or scheduled meeting. Do not transform those dates into a limitations calculation. Filing and response rules vary according to law, forum, employer, place, and facts. Verify directly with current instructions and qualified counsel.

Checks 4–6: organize the evidence without overcollecting

4. Make an evidence index. Assign a simple identifier to each item and list its date, author, recipient, subject, file type, source, and storage location. Common categories include offer and policy documents, evaluations, schedules, wage statements, time records, leave communications, accommodation requests, complaints, investigation messages, discipline, termination papers, and agreements. Preserve full conversation threads where available, not only excerpts that appear helpful. 5. Separate originals, working copies, and recollections. Keep downloaded or exported originals unchanged. Use duplicates for highlighting, redaction, and annotations. Store personal notes in a clearly labeled folder so they are not mistaken for employer records. When a verbal conversation matters, write a dated recollection stating when the note was created, who participated, what was remembered, and which parts remain uncertain. Never backdate a note. 6. Check lawful access and privacy. Do not bypass passwords, forward material contrary to lawful restrictions, or take files the employee has no right to possess. Avoid collecting unrelated trade secrets, customer data, medical information, or coworker personnel records. If a potentially relevant document is inaccessible, identify it by description, date range, likely holder, and reason it matters. That notation can be more responsible than improper acquisition. For wage questions, include actual duties, control, pay periods, rates, deductions, and time worked. The Department of Labor's FLSA material and employment-relationship fact sheet provide federal concepts, but labels such as “contractor,” “manager,” or “salaried” do not alone resolve coverage or classification. For leave, retain notices, requests, certification correspondence, attendance entries, and job descriptions; the federal FMLA employee guide applies only where its coverage conditions are met.

Checks 7–8: prepare the question and secure handoff

7. Draft a one-page briefing sheet. Include the neutral issue sentence, five to ten pivotal events, involved decision-makers, work locations, current employment status, known upcoming dates, internal steps already taken, and the precise help requested. Add a “missing or disputed” box. Examples include an unavailable policy, conflicting time totals, an unclear reason for discipline, or uncertainty about who received a complaint. Keep theories in a separate questions list rather than presenting them as established findings. 8. Prepare a secure sharing plan. Ask the intended recipient how files should be transmitted before emailing sensitive material. Use descriptive filenames, a folder index, and the smallest relevant set for an initial review. Keep a local inventory of what was shared, when, with whom, and by what method. Redact only on working copies and disclose that a redaction was made. Retain complete originals safely. Finish with three prioritized questions. One might ask which jurisdictional facts require confirmation. Another might ask which missing record is most important. A third might ask what immediate process choice needs attention. Avoid asking a reviewer to guarantee representation, contingency terms, timing, damages, settlement, or verdict. Those outcomes cannot responsibly be promised from a preparation packet. The checklist is complete when a new reader can follow the sequence, locate the supporting records, see what remains uncertain, and understand the next decision. It is not complete merely because the folder is large. Concise, authentic, well-indexed material usually communicates more clearly than an unfiltered archive. Revisit the packet when a new event occurs, preserving prior versions so the evolution of the record remains visible.

Questions this guide answers

Should an employee send every available workplace file before a consultation?

Usually the better first step is a relevant, indexed set delivered through the recipient's secure method. Keep a complete inventory and preserve originals.

How should a verbal event appear in the packet?

Create a dated recollection identifying when it was written, participants, remembered details, and uncertainties. Never present a later note as contemporaneous.

What belongs on a jurisdiction card?

Record work locations, employer location, remote-work facts, known workforce information, employment setting, relevant agreements, and pending dates without deciding which law applies.
