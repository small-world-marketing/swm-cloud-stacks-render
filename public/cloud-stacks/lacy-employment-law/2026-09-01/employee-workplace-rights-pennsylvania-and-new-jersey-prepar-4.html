employee workplace rights Pennsylvania and New Jersey: Preparation Checklist 3

A practical checklist for building a clean chronology, document index, pay or leave records, question list, and consultation packet.

Preparation is a filtering exercise, not a race to collect every workplace file. A strong packet lets the recipient understand the employer, work location, central events, available proof, missing facts, and requested help without searching an unstructured archive. The decision addressed here is what belongs in a usable intake or consultation package—and what should stay out. Employees should preserve only material they may lawfully access, protect medical and coworker information, and avoid altering originals. The packet should remain useful whether the next conversation concerns discrimination, retaliation, wages, classification, family leave, disability accommodation, severance, a non-compete, or termination. It does not establish wrongdoing and cannot replace a lawyer’s review of individual facts or agreements. This checklist is informational, not legal advice. Its review date is September 1, 2026; confirm current agency and provider requirements before using it later.

Checklist A: create the core record set

□ Write a two-sentence purpose statement. Name the immediate question and desired assistance, such as understanding a leave response, checking missing overtime records, evaluating a severance document, or deciding where to report specific conduct. Avoid opening with a legal verdict. □ Build a chronology. Use columns for date or range, event, participants, exact words if genuinely remembered, witness, related file, worker response, and unresolved question. Mark estimates. Include earlier context and later consequences only when they help explain the central issue. □ Verify identity and jurisdiction facts. Record the employer’s legal name from a reliable employment document, worksite, job title, employment dates, supervisor, employing or staffing entities, and the states where work occurred. Do not guess workforce size or corporate relationships; label unknowns. □ Preserve communications. Save relevant emails, texts, letters, portal notices, meeting invitations, complaints, acknowledgments, and responses in their original form where possible. Keep metadata and complete threads. A screenshot should show enough surrounding information to identify sender, recipient, date, and context. □ Index employment documents. Potentially relevant items include offer materials, handbook provisions, policies, evaluations, discipline, schedules, time records, pay statements, job descriptions, leave notices, accommodation correspondence, separation papers, restrictive covenants, and amendments. Relevance depends on the question. Do not remove entire personnel databases or proprietary collections. □ Create a witness list based on knowledge, not loyalty. For each person, state what the individual directly observed and how contact information was obtained. Do not coach accounts or coordinate stories. □ Maintain a preservation log. Note where each item came from, when it was saved, whether it is complete, and where the original remains. Use secure storage outside employer-controlled equipment when lawful. Do not change timestamps, crop away context, or annotate the only copy.

Checklist B: add issue-specific modules

□ For pay or classification, assemble pay periods, hours actually worked, schedules, time-entry edits, rate information, duties performed, instructions, and control over the work. The Department of Labor’s FLSA page introduces federal wage-and-hour subjects, while its employment-relationship fact sheet explains that labels alone do not decide federal employee status. Do not assume that either source resolves Pennsylvania or New Jersey requirements. □ For discrimination or harassment, identify the employment decision or conduct, dates, people involved, protected characteristic believed relevant, statements or patterns supporting that belief, known comparators, reporting history, and employer response. Separate firsthand facts from workplace rumor. The EEOC’s filing page describes what a charge asks an agency to investigate, but preparation does not prove coverage or discrimination. □ For retaliation, distinguish the earlier action—such as communicating about suspected discrimination or participating in a process—from the later response. Add dates, decision-makers, explanations provided, and records showing performance or treatment before and after. EEOC material explains protected activity and also notes that legitimate discipline remains possible. □ For family or medical leave, collect requests, eligibility notices, certification communications, designation notices, dates absent, benefit information, and return-to-work correspondence. Keep medical material in a restricted subfolder. Consult the federal employee guide for the FMLA framework, then verify applicability. □ For disability accommodation, organize the request, functional workplace limitation, proposed changes, employer questions, medical documentation actually supplied, and interactive communications. Avoid adding unrelated medical history. □ For severance or non-compete review, provide the complete signed and unsigned agreements, amendments, compensation documents, job description, proposed response date, and the worker’s priorities. Do not edit clauses, sign based on a checklist, or ask a general article to interpret enforceability.

Checklist C: package it for an efficient review

□ Draft a one-page summary containing the current employment status, central question, five pivotal events, desired outcome, approaching dates shown on documents, and a list of attached records. Label potential agency periods as “requires verification” rather than calculating them. □ Prepare focused questions. Examples include: Which jurisdictions and forums require analysis? What facts are missing? What route fits the immediate objective? Does the reviewer need the complete policy or agreement? What work would a proposed engagement include? Who monitors dates before representation is confirmed? □ Create a document index with neutral file names, dates, short descriptions, and page counts. Arrange records chronologically within issue folders. Deduplicate exact copies but preserve distinct versions. Never send passwords in the same message as protected files. □ Redact cautiously. Ask the recipient about secure transfer and necessary identifiers before distributing Social Security numbers, financial account details, children’s data, medical information, or unrelated coworker records. Preserve an untouched original separately. □ Record gaps. Missing pay periods, unavailable messages, uncertain dates, or incomplete agreements should appear on a gap list. Honest gaps are more useful than reconstructed facts presented as certain. □ Recheck current instructions. Agencies and providers may impose their own intake formats. Deadlines can differ by statute, forum, employer, location, and facts. Consult present agency guidance and qualified counsel; never personalize a filing period from this checklist. The packet is ready when a new reader can identify the decision, follow the sequence, locate each important record, and see uncertainty without receiving an indiscriminate data dump. Keep a transmission log showing what was sent, to whom, how, and when.

Questions this guide answers

Should a worker collect every file available at work?

No. Preserve relevant material the worker may lawfully access, maintain context, and avoid taking proprietary, confidential, or unrelated collections merely because they are accessible.

How should uncertain dates appear in a chronology?

Mark them as estimates, explain the basis for the estimate, and list the record that might confirm the date. Do not present reconstructed details as certain.

What makes a consultation packet usable?

A concise summary, factual timeline, issue-specific records, document index, focused questions, and visible information gaps let a reviewer understand the matter efficiently.
