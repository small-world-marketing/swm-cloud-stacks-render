What Work Facts Can Help You Ask About Classification?

Record the terms and actual working arrangements that qualified counsel or an agency may need for a classification enquiry.

Reviewed November 23, 2026. This article provides general legal information. Consult qualified legal counsel for advice about your circumstances.

Prepare a classification question with the records describing your work and a separate account of how tasks were actually assigned and carried out. Put the dates, locations and organizations involved at the top. The US Department of Labor publishes information about employment relationships under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act. Its current materials may refer to changes and dates that deserve careful checking. Ask qualified counsel or the relevant agency which rules apply to your circumstances and when. This worksheet helps identify evidence and questions without determining someone’s status or applying a legal test to a reader's situation.

What records describe the arrangement?

Find the agreement, communication or other record that identifies the role and relevant time period. Record its date and exact title and preserve the version you can appropriately retain. If there were amendments or later arrangements, put them on their own lines. Ask qualified counsel how to address missing or restricted material. Keep written statements separate from your account of the work. A reviewer may want information about what happened in practice during a specific period. An accurate document index helps the person identify which language and dates belong to that period without treating a label by itself as a conclusion.

How were the day-to-day assignments organized?

Describe who communicated the work to you, the context and the dates you can confirm. Record whether the way work was actually handled changed over time. Use plain factual descriptions and avoid recasting an observation as the answer to a legal test. Include the location of duties and the organization or contact that communicated decisions as known. Consider a small number of examples that show how the arrangement worked. Keep confidential details and third-party information protected. Ask counsel which aspects are relevant to the law or question it is reviewing and how any records should be collected.

What can you verify from pay and service records?

Identify recurring pay documents, invoices or statements you are permitted to access and retain. Write the period and entity attached to them. Keep figures in their original context and label any estimate. If you handled expenses or received relevant instructions, record the documents supporting those facts. Do not reach a classification conclusion from how one record refers to the role. Tell the reviewer what is confirmed and what remains unknown. Ask which records could clarify the situation. A qualified person can address the implications for a particular legal area, which may require facts outside the initial pay record.

Which law and time period does your question concern?

Write the work dates, location and the practical reason for asking. Current requirements and agency guidance may change, and different laws can use distinct approaches. The Labor Department fact sheet describes its federal wage-law context. Ask the relevant authority or qualified counsel to determine which current framework applies to your facts and time period. Keep a source and date with the response. Avoid applying an example from another forum to your own situation. If timing could matter, get advice promptly before acting on a generalized description. Include the relevant employment or agreement deadlines in your questions as appropriate.

What can you share appropriately to obtain advice?

Prepare a factual summary and an index of available documents. Ask the recipient what information it needs and how you should provide it. If a document is restricted, seek qualified advice about handling it before copying or sending it. Limit what you share to what the process reasonably needs and avoid transferring private records about other people. A missing document can be identified with a description or source where appropriate. Keep the facts and questions separate so the adviser understands both how work was done and which decision or concern you need help examining.

How will you track the next step?

Record the question, person contacted and relevant period. Ask whether another source or adviser needs to be involved and what information it will require. Confirm which action the recipient has agreed to take. If the facts or work arrangements change, note the change with its date and ask whether it affects any guidance you received. Save legal advice or agency information in an appropriate private record. A clear sequence can help you seek the next qualified opinion without assuming that one answer applies to a different assignment, employer or period.

Keep the support documents with the question they address.
